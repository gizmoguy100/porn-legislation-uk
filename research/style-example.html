01 · Key findings

Four numbers, one problem

The Online Safety Act did not ban pornography. It built a gate. The gate is real — the top ten UK porn sites now ask for age. But a gate is only as strong as its hinges, and in 2026 four of them are failing at once.

Those numbers describe the same problem from different angles. The duty is on the books. The regulator is active. The top of the market has moved. But the tail has not, the discovery layer has not, and the money has not.

This report is diagnostic. It does not propose a fix. It maps the places where the regime’s reach exceeds its grip.

02 · The duty

A single sentence, doing a lot of work

Part 5 of the Online Safety Act 2023 applies to an internet service if three conditions are met. It must publish or display “regulated provider pornographic content”; it must not be exempt; and it must have links with the UK. If all three hold, s 81 imposes the duty.

04 s 81 age-assurance duty HEAA
Online Safety Act 2023, s 81(2)–(3)

A duty to ensure, by the use of age verification or age estimation (or both), that children are not normally able to encounter content that is regulated provider pornographic content in relation to the service.

The age verification or age estimation must be of such a kind, and used in such a way, that it is highly effective at correctly determining whether or not a particular user is a child.

The duty is not a ban. It is an outcome: children not “normally” able to encounter the content. Neither “normally” nor “highly effective” is defined in the statute. Ofcom supplies four criteria — technically accurate, robust, reliable, fair — but they are guidance, not law, and they carry no numeric threshold.

That is the first vulnerability, and it is structural. The Act delegates the hardest question — what counts as effective — to a regulator, which in turn delegates it to providers, which choose their own methods.

03 · Enforcement

The fines are real. The collection is not.

Ofcom has issued 31 fines against 11 providers, totalling more than £7m. Only two providers have paid — about £135,000, or roughly 1.9 per cent of the nominal total. The rest is in dispute, in appeal, or simply unpaid.

The regulator’s own words

“We will not be able to recover every single fine… If foreign courts decide to try to get in our way, there will be a point at which we have to accept that we will not be able to recover that money.”

Dame Melanie Dawes, Ofcom chief executive, to the DCMS Committee, 20 October 2026.

Geo-blocking has become the functional equivalent of compliance. Ofcom counts a UK block as a mitigation; in its July 2026 scorecard, 10 of the top 100 services had blocked the UK, 64 had age assurance, and 26 had neither. A block is not an age check. It is an exit — and a VPN walks around it.

04 · The search hole

The discovery layer is outside the duty

The clearest statutory gap is not in enforcement. It is in the definition. Section 79(6)(b) says porn that “appears in search results” is not “published or displayed” by the search service. Search engines are therefore not Part 5 porn publishers, however prominently they surface the content.

They are regulated search services under Part 3, with duties to minimise the risk of children encountering primary priority content. But that is a different, softer standard. Ofcom’s own report says the quiet part out loud.

Search services are not required under the Act to use highly effective age assurance to prevent access to pornographic content by children.

Ofcom, Use of Age Assurance Report 2026, note 9

The practical effect is a conveyor belt. In Ofcom’s study, 62 per cent of children who visited porn used a search engine immediately beforehand. The destination sites face the hard duty; the discovery layer does not.

05 · Case study

4chan: two events, one ruling

“The 4chan ruling” is usually compressed into a single story. It is two events on two continents, and only one is a ruling.

10 June 2025

Ofcom opens the investigation

Case CW/01307/06/25, covering information notices, the illegal-content risk assessment and the age-assurance duty.

13 October 2025

£20,000 for information-notice breaches

4chan failed to supply its risk-assessment record and qualifying worldwide revenue data.

19 March 2026

£520,000 in fixed penalties

£450,000 for the s 12 age-assurance failure, £50,000 for the risk assessment, £20,000 for the terms of service. Compliance deadline: 2 April 2026.

15 September 2026

The US court dismisses 4chan and Kiwi Farms’ suit

The D.D.C. holds that Ofcom is a “foreign state” under the FSIA. The merits are never reached.

20 October 2026

Ofcom tells MPs the money is not arriving

Nine of 11 online-safety fines remain unpaid; four providers have geo-blocked the UK; three services remain available.

Ofcom’s decision is an administrative finding. The US judgment is a jurisdictional dismissal. The court did not uphold the fines, and it did not strike them down. It held that Ofcom is a foreign state under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, so the suit could not proceed in Washington. The merits were never reached.

What the court actually said

“Plaintiffs’ suit fails because they seek to use American courts as a sword against Ofcom rather than as a shield to protect themselves.”

Judge Rudolph Contreras, D.D.C., 15 September 2026.

The obligation layer survived. The collection layer did not. 4chan has no UK staff, servers or assets; nothing has been paid. The only realistic UK lever is a geo-block, which secures neither payment nor an age check.

06 · Register

Where the statute leaks

Severity is an editorial judgement, not a statutory classification. The evidence column is the part that matters.

Vulnerability Provision Severity Evidence
Search results excluded s 79(6)(b) High 33% of Google and 54% of Bing first-page results led to unprotected sites (Ofcom, July 2026).
UGC carve-out s 79(7) High Tube, cam and fan sites fall under Part 3, with a later timetable and a softer standard.
“Significant number” undefined s 80(4) High Providers self-assess scope; Ofcom has never published a numeric threshold.
“Normally” / “highly effective” s 81(2)–(3) High No metric in statute; the four criteria are guidance only; no enforcement for circumvention.
Fines uncollectable Sch 13 High 9 of 11 unpaid; 4 geo-blocked; 3 still available (October 2026).
Identity binding s 81(2) High The check verifies that someone is 18, not that the current user is that person.
Privacy duty is procedural s 81(4)(b) Medium “Have regard” only; no minimisation, retention or security standard.
App stores and devices outside — Medium Ofcom says app stores “could do more”; they are not regulated services.
AI companions and nudify tools s 79(2) Medium 163 of 181 nudify sites were accessible from the UK; only 2 had age verification (ISD).
