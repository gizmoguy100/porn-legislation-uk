# Dossier 03 — Circumvention, identity-binding and regulator responses

**Prepared:** 2 November 2026
**Purpose:** evidence gathering only (no analysis for the final report)
**Scope:** policy, law, regulation, prevalence data and news reporting on how children get around the UK age-verification regime for pornography, and how institutions have responded.

**Deliberately excluded:** exploit mechanics (cookies, tokens, `postMessage`, browser extensions, step-by-step spoofing). Where researchers or journalists defeated a check, this dossier records only the headline, the policy significance and the source.

**Framework assumed:**
- Part 5, s 81 OSA 2023 — regulated pornography providers must use "highly effective" age assurance so that children are not "normally" able to encounter regulated porn. In force **17 January 2025**.
- Part 3 children's safety duties (ss 11–12) — in force **25 July 2025**; s 36 children's access assessments in force **16 April 2025**.
- Ofcom "highly effective age assurance" (HEAA) guidance — statement published **April 2025**.

**Note on dates:** the brief refers to a "Lords debate (Nov 2025)". The substantive Lords debate on VPNs was **15 September 2025**; there was a follow-up oral question on **4 December 2025**; and a Lords division on a VPN amendment on **26 January 2026**. A widely circulated law-firm summary is dated 14 November 2025 and describes the September debate. All three parliamentary events are recorded below.

## 1. VPN use and location spoofing

VPNs let a user appear to browse from another country. Because much UK age assurance is geo-applied (checks switched on for UK IP addresses), a VPN terminating outside the UK can present a user to a service as a non-UK visitor, outside the age-check flow. This is the single most politically salient circumvention route, and the evidence on its scale is genuinely contested.

### What we found

**Ofcom's statutory report (15 July 2026) — VPN use doubled and stayed higher**

- Source: Ofcom, *Report on the use of age assurance* (published 15 July 2026; PDF updated 27 July 2026), para 7.67.
- URL: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/online-safety/information-for-industry/age-assurance-report-2026/report-on-the-use-of-age-assurance.pdf
- Quote: "Our latest analysis shows a sustained higher level of VPN use since the initial spike, with the estimated number of daily UK VPN users being **2.2 million compared with 1.2 million daily users prior to 25 July 2025**."
- Method note in the report: Similarweb daily active users for VPN mobile apps, iOS and Android, extracted 7 May 2026; Ofcom concedes "it is not possible, using this dataset, to establish the proportion of this increase that is driven by children."

**The earlier, much-quoted "1.5 million" figure**

- Source: Ofcom Chief Executive Dame Melanie Dawes, quoted in ISPreview, 26 January 2026 (and originally November 2025).
- URL: https://www.ispreview.co.uk/index.php/2026/01/house-of-lords-votes-to-ban-uk-children-from-using-internet-vpns.html
- Quote: "following the 25th July deadline we saw a spike in [VPN] use – with UK daily active users of VPN apps temporarily doubling to around 1.5 million. However, usage has since plateaued, and has now fallen back to around 1 million by the end of September."
- Why it matters: the public record contains **two different Ofcom baselines and two different peaks** (1.5m falling to ~1m in late 2025; 2.2m sustained vs 1.2m in mid-2026, on a different data supplier). Ofcom's 2026 report explicitly says it "updates Ofcom's previous view which was based on a different data supplier". Any report should not treat "VPN use doubled" as a single stable official statistic.

**Ofcom's own conclusion is cautious**

- Source: Ofcom report 2026, para 7.64.
- Quote: "Our evidence suggests that VPN use has increased among UK users, but **does not currently show that this increase is due to children motivated by a desire to bypass age checks** on online services."

**Government-commissioned prevalence study (14 July 2026)**

- Source: DSIT/BMG Research, *Children's circumvention behaviours online*, published 14 July 2026, fieldwork 18–29 May 2026, n=2,299 aged 11–17 (plus VPN boost of 270).
- URL: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/childrens-circumvention-behaviours-online/childrens-circumvention-behaviours-online
- Key figures:
  - 58% have heard of VPNs; **26% have ever used one; 22% in the last 3 months**.
  - Lifetime use rises from 17% (11–12) to 31% (16–17).
  - 64% of lifetime users use one at least weekly.
  - **22% of VPN users say they use one to reach age-restricted sites/apps/games — equivalent to 7% of all children.**
  - **10% of all children say they bypassed a check using "settings or tools that change their apparent location"** (27% of those who bypassed).
  - 56% of lifetime VPN users have successfully bypassed a check, vs 33% of never-users; **71% among children who set up the VPN themselves**.
  - Parental involvement: 22% of lifetime users were helped to set it up by a parent; 43% say a parent pays; 36% use a free VPN.
  - 54% of current/lifetime users started within the last 12 months.

**Ofcom's own children's VPN study says 5%, and flags reporting bias**

- Source: Ofcom report 2026, para 7.72, citing Ofcom, *Children's Use of VPNs* (March 2026, GB 11–17).
- Quote: "**5% of children in this study said they had used a VPN in the past six months** to either access content meant for people older than them, or to access features and functions with age restrictions. In a separate study, a similar proportion (5%) told us they had circumvented highly effective age restrictions in the past 12 months. **Both studies may understate the proportion of children circumventing these restrictions due to response bias**, such as a reluctance to disclose this behaviour to a researcher."
- The same paragraph notes Internet Matters' May 2026 finding that 7% of children who bypassed did so via VPN.

**A striking internal inconsistency worth flagging**

- Source: Ofcom report 2026, para 7.70, citing February 2026 research.
- Quote: "three quarters (**77%**) of 16 to 17 year olds say they have ever used a VPN, with this rising to nearly nine in ten (**87%**) among those who are active users of online pornography."
- Problem: this sits alongside Ofcom's March 2026 finding of 31% lifetime VPN use among 16–17s (para 7.68) and the DSIT figure of 31%. A 77% figure for the same age group is an outlier by a factor of ~2.5 and appears to come from a pornography-user panel. Treat with caution; do not use as a population estimate.

**Independent research says children's VPN use is flat**

- Source: Internet Matters, "New data shows no rise in children's VPN use after the introduction of online age checks", 4 December 2025 (Pulse survey, n=1,000, 9–17, Oct–Nov 2025).
- URL: https://www.internetmatters.org/hub/research/data-shows-no-rise-childrens-vpn-use-amid-online-age-checks/
- Figures: **8% used a VPN in the past 12 months; "no statistically significant changes … over the past two years"**; 5% of 11–12s, 10% of 13–14s, 11% of 15–17s; 66% cite data protection, 34% restricted entertainment; **only 16% of VPN users cite restricted adult content**.
- Same finding echoed by Childnet (December 2025), cited in the Ofcom report at para 7.71 (21% of 8–17s have used a VPN; no post-July-2025 spike attributable to children).

**App-store evidence of a demand spike (adults and children not separated)**

- Source: European Parliamentary Research Service, *Virtual private networks and the protection of children online*, PE 782.618, January 2026.
- URL: https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/ATAG/2026/782618/EPRS_ATA(2026)782618_EN.pdf
- Quote: "Since the related legislation came into force in 2025, **half of the top 10 free apps in app-download charts in UK app stores have reportedly been VPN services. One app developer reported a 1,800% spike in downloads** in the first month after the legislation started to apply."

**Government position: no ban, no age-gating — but "prepared to take further action"**

- Source: DSIT, *Growing up in the online world: government response*, July 2026, section 3.2 ("Circumvention").
- URL: https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/growing-up-in-the-online-world-a-national-consultation/outcome/growing-up-in-the-online-world-government-response-july-2026
- Quotes:
  - "**We will not ban or age-gate VPNs.** However, we must ensure that these tools are not misused to undermine child safety protections and we will keep this area under close review. **We are prepared to take further action on this, should evidence show that circumvention is not being tackled adequately.**"
  - "Government has asked Ofcom to conduct research into what additional steps services can take to detect and mitigate circumvention on their platforms, including via VPNs."
  - "…call on VPN providers to engage with government on making their products safer and less accessible to children without parental consent."
  - "Requiring everyone to undergo age checks to access a VPN would impose significant compliance costs, affect the privacy of millions of legitimate adult users, and create new security vulnerabilities."
- Consultation numbers: 38% of respondents prioritised more education for children, vs 34% who prioritised restricting children's access to VPNs.

**Earlier ministerial statements to the same effect**

- Source: Hansard, HL Deb, 15 September 2025, *Online Safety Act 2023: Virtual Private Networks* (Lord Leong for the Government), as summarised by Mishcon de Reya, 14 November 2025.
- URL (debate): https://hansard.parliament.uk/lords/2025-09-15/debates/57714CE6-0CE4-49F6-B028-E271D5100F7F/OnlineSafetyAct2023VirtualPrivateNetworks
- URL (summary): https://www.mishcon.com/news/online-safety-act-vpns-and-age-verification-what-the-house-of-lords-debate-reveals
- Points: providers must assess circumvention risk in their children's access assessments; a **"ban on apps promoting VPN use is not being considered"** because of legitimate uses; Ofcom would report on effectiveness by June 2026.
- Baroness Kidron (crossbench) in the same debate: no evidence the reported VPN increase was wholly attributable to children; Ofcom guidance suggests **only about one in ten VPN users is a child**.

**Enforcement against VPN *promoters* — a threat, not (yet) an action**

- Source: Hansard, HL Deb, 4 December 2025, *Children: Age Verification and Virtual Private Networks*, col. 11am–11.30am (Baroness Lloyd of Effra).
- URL: https://hansard.parliament.uk/lords/2025-12-04/debates/4ECC433F-7DB4-43ED-845B-687DFE5D8B50/ChildrenAgeVerificationAndVirtualPrivateNetworks
- Quote: "Services must also take steps to mitigate against circumvention methods that are easily accessible to children. **Providers that do not comply with their child safety duties by deliberately promoting the use of VPNs could face enforcement action under the Act.**"
- Also: "There is currently limited evidence on how many children use VPNs, and the Government are addressing this evidence gap."
- Source: UK Government spokesperson, 31 July 2025, reported by ISPreview.
- URL: https://www.ispreview.co.uk/index.php/2025/07/uk-government-warns-promoting-the-use-of-vpns-could-attract-fines.html
- Quote: "Where platforms **deliberately target UK children and promote VPN use**, they could face enforcement action, including significant financial penalties."

### Status

- Policy settled (for now): **no VPN ban, no VPN age-gating** (July 2026). The live commitments are research (Ofcom), engagement with VPN providers, and parent-facing guidance.
- Political pressure exists but has not converted into law: a Lords amendment to the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Bill requiring a prohibition on providing VPN services to under-18s was carried on **26 January 2026 by 207 votes to 159** against Government opposition (amendment 92, moved by Lord Nash). The Government subsequently restated that it would not ban VPNs.
- No enforcement action has been taken against any VPN provider, VPN app, influencer or affiliate marketer. The only legal hook is the OSA duty not to *promote* circumvention (see §5), which binds regulated services, not VPN vendors.
- Ofcom's own data shows a real, sustained increase in UK VPN use but cannot attribute it to children; its child-specific estimate is 5%, with an explicit reporting-bias caveat. Independent surveys find no child-specific spike.

### Gaps / unknowns

- No official estimate of the **proportion of UK porn traffic that arrives via VPN**. Ofcom's Similarweb data is app-level, not destination-level.
- No published measurement of how many children reach *regulated* porn services via VPN as opposed to simply migrating to unregulated sites (the "displacement" problem, which Ofcom says is the bigger issue — see §3 and §5).
- The 77%/87% Ofcom figure for 16–17s is unexplained and unreconciled with the 31% figures; needs a direct query to Ofcom before use.
- No data on **who** is in the 2.2m daily VPN users (age, purpose, whether the VPN is household-level).
- Unclear whether the January 2026 Lords VPN clause survived into the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 April 2026); the July 2026 policy statement implies it did not, but the Act text was not verified for this dossier.
- The Government has not published terms of reference or a timetable for the Ofcom VPN-detection research it commissioned in July 2026.

## 2. Borrowed identity, device and account

The regime verifies *a* person, not *the* person. The dominant real-world circumvention route is not technical at all: a child uses an adult's face, ID, login, device or payment card, often with the adult's knowledge. This is the area where the statutory "not normally able to encounter" test is weakest, because the check succeeds.

### What we found

**Ofcom names the mechanism explicitly**

- Source: Ofcom report 2026, para 7.76.
- Quote: "One of the ways children may circumvent age assurance is by **using accounts belonging to adults, or by relying on an adult to complete an age check on their behalf**. For example, a parent or older sibling may complete an age check so that a child can access specific content, or they may allow their personal details to be used, such as an ID document or credit card details. In some cases, children may have an account that is **shared with an adult who has completed an age check** and therefore the child has unrestricted access to a service."
- Para 7.77 (Adults' Media Lives): a participant said children "will just get someone else to log in on their behalf"; another described how easy it is for a child to ask a parent to take a photo for the check.

**Government research quantifies "posing as someone else"**

- Source: DSIT/BMG, *Children's circumvention behaviours online*, 14 July 2026.
- Figures:
  - **63% of children who bypassed a check did so by "posing as someone else"** — equivalent to **24% of all children**.
  - Of those who pretended to be old enough: **45% gave a false date of birth; 11% used a parent's or relative's ID; 5% used someone else's ID; 6% used altered images or disguises**.
  - 50% learned how to bypass from friends their own age; 21% worked it out themselves; **10% learned from a parent or guardian**.
- Analytic point: the single largest circumvention category in official data is identity substitution, not VPNs (10%).

**Internet Matters puts numbers on parental facilitation**

- Source: Internet Matters, *The Online Safety Act: Are children safer online?*, May 2026 (n≈1,000 children aged 9–16, plus 7 focus groups).
- URL (report page): https://www.internetmatters.org/hub/research/online-safety-act-report-2026/
- URL (PDF): https://www.internetmatters.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Internet-Matters-Online-Safety-Act-Report-May-2026.pdf
- Figures:
  - **26% of parents have allowed their child to bypass age checks; 17% actively helped; 9% "allowed it or turned a blind eye".**
  - 32% of children say they have bypassed a check in the past two months.
  - Methods: fake birthday 13%; **someone else's login 9%; someone else's device 8%; someone else's ID 6%; random photo 6%; VPN 7%**.
  - Parent quote: "I have helped my son get around them. It was to play a game, and I knew the game, and I was happy and confident that I was fine with him playing it."
- Note: the "one in six parents have helped" framing used in press coverage (e.g. *The Independent*, 1 May 2026) is the 17% "actively helped" figure, not the 26% "allowed".

**The "adult at some point" problem**

- The brief's phrase "someone over 18 was involved at some point" was **not found verbatim** in any official source. The substantive point is well evidenced in three places: Ofcom para 7.76 (adult completes the check / shared account); DSIT (11% parental ID, 63% posing as someone else); and Internet Matters (26% parental permission, 9% someone else's login, 8% someone else's device).
- Ofcom's own guidance treats this as a design requirement, not an inevitability: see §5 on verifying *ownership* of ID, phone, email and card, and on repeat checks "to prevent child access via device or account sharing" (HEAA guidance, para 4.35).

**Ofcom found services relying on a single, one-time check**

- Source: Ofcom report 2026, Section 8 summary table (Figure A1).
- Quote: "Many services rely on a one-time age check and do not require users to repeat age checks." Ofcom's recommended improvement: repeat checks and/or "targeted and proportionate anti-circumvention measures after an initial age gate", including triggers such as "suspicious behaviour or new log ins".

**A live enforcement case turns on identity-binding across the ecosystem**

- Source: Ofcom, investigation into Aylo Freesites Ltd (Pornhub), opened 22 September 2026, case CW/01358/09/26.
- URL: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/investigation-into-aylos-compliance-with-the-duty-to-prevent-children-from-encountering-pornographic-content-through-the-use-of-age-assurance
- Quote: "We are concerned that Aylo may not have conducted sufficient due diligence and testing before implementing a new age assurance process on Pornhub in May 2026… The process **relies on signals from a third-party (Apple)** that suggest UK iOS/iPadOS users attempting to access Pornhub may have completed Apple's age checks."
- Significance: the first enforcement case that tests whether an *inherited* assurance signal (device/app-store level) is genuinely "highly effective" at binding age to the actual user. Ofcom expressly says it will examine Aylo's integration and due diligence, not Apple's checks.

### Status

- Parental and adult facilitation is now **official, quantified and acknowledged by the regulator** — no longer a fringe claim.
- It is nonetheless not the subject of any enforcement action or specific guidance beyond generic "verify ownership" and "repeat checks" expectations.
- No provider has been sanctioned for accepting an adult's ID/face where the service otherwise followed guidance.

### Gaps / unknowns

- No data on **account/credential resale** in the UK (verified accounts sold or shared for porn access). Searches found only general warnings in other jurisdictions (e.g. Australia's eSafety FAQ on "direct access to an age-verified account"). This is a genuine evidence gap.
- No official estimate of what share of "successful" bypasses involved an adult, as opposed to a child fabricating details.
- No data on **device sharing** within households (a parent's unlocked phone or tablet) as distinct from account sharing.
- Unclear how Ofcom would treat parental facilitation: is a parent completing a check a "circumvention technique easily accessible to children" that the provider must mitigate, or outside the provider's control? Not addressed in published guidance.
- No assessment of whether the s 81 "not normally" threshold is met when access depends on an adult's cooperation.

## 3. Prevalence data

The headline numbers vary by a factor of two or more depending on question wording, sample and whether self-declaration checks are included. This is the most important methodological caveat in the whole evidence base: **"39% of children got around an age check" is not the same as "39% got around highly effective age assurance on pornography".**

### What we found

**GOV.UK / DSIT — the most detailed official study (14 July 2026)**

- Source: DSIT/BMG Research, *Children's circumvention behaviours online*, 14 July 2026. n=2,299, 11–17, UK, fieldwork 18–29 May 2026.
- Figures:
  - 78% have encountered some form of age check; 19% have never seen one.
  - Checks encountered: tick box 57%; date of birth 56%; facial age estimation 26%; government ID 17%; payment card 17%; third-party ID app 14%.
  - **53% have deliberately chosen a platform to avoid a check** (37% because it had no check; 34% because it would be easy to get around).
  - **53% have attempted to get around a check; 39% succeeded; 14% tried and failed.**
  - Success: 28% of 11–12s; 43% of 13–15s and 16–17s.
  - **63% of bypassers posed as someone else (24% of all children); 10% of all children used location-changing tools.**
  - Most reported bypassing concerned **self-declaration checks**: 73% of bypassers had met a tick box; 71% a date-of-birth prompt; 45% used a false date of birth.
  - 60% of bypassers had also met at least one advanced check (facial age estimation 37%; government ID 24%).
  - 51% of bypassers say they encountered at least one type of harmful content afterwards (20% of all children); 35% of those who saw explicit sexual content say they saw it **only after** bypassing.
  - Deterrents rated highest: checks that are harder to get around (44%), permanent account ban (40%), parent notification (39%).
- Ofcom's own summary of the study: "Circumvention appears low, although some activity may not be captured due to reporting bias" (report 2026, Overview).

**Internet Matters — the most widely reported NGO survey (May 2026)**

- Source: *The Online Safety Act: Are children safer online?*, May 2026.
- Figures: **46% think age checks are easy to bypass; 17% think difficult; 32% have bypassed in the past two months; 13% fake birthday; 9% someone else's login; 8% someone else's device; 7% VPN; 6% someone else's ID; 6% random photo; 3% "I don't know"**.
- 49% of children say they experienced harm online in the past month; 53% were recently asked to verify age; 68% see more safety features.
- Ofcom's response to the report: "Weak or easily bypassed age checks are not good enough" (quoted in *The Independent*, 1 May 2026).

**Ofcom's statutory report — circumvention "appears low" but the bigger problem is displacement (15 July 2026)**

- Source: Ofcom report 2026, Overview and Section 4.
- Figures:
  - **8% of children attempt to access pornography; half of those only reached sites with age checks in place.**
  - 87% of children's visits to porn sites lasted under 30 seconds; 65% under 10 seconds.
  - **47% of the pornography services visited by children had no age checks in place.**
  - 64 of the top 100 UK porn services had age assurance by June 2026; a further 10 geo-block the UK.
  - 62% of children who visited porn used a search engine immediately beforehand; **33% of first-page Google results and 54% of Bing results for relevant queries were to porn sites without age checks**.
  - 5% of children used a VPN to access age-restricted content in the past six months; 5% said they had circumvented HEAA in the past 12 months; both "may understate" due to response bias (para 7.72).
  - 16% of 13–17s know how to circumvent highly effective age gates, but only 5% had done so in the past 12 months (para 7.76, citing *Children and Parents: Media Use and Attitudes*).

**The displacement finding is arguably the most important prevalence result**

- Ofcom's own framing: age assurance is "proving effective at the individual service level when deployed correctly and in line with our HEAA guidance, but **not yet at an overall system level, due to user migration to non-age assured sites**" (para 3.21(a)).
- Structural gap: **search services are not required under the Act to use HEAA to prevent access to pornographic content by children** (Ofcom press release, note 9). Google and Bing have agreed to "explore practical solutions" on discoverability, not to age-gate.

**Survey-quality warning**

- DSIT: "Evidence presented in this report relies on self-reported behaviours from both parents and children… there may be reasons why some behaviours are over- or under-reported." The questionnaire deliberately used "settings or tools that change a child's apparent location" rather than "VPN" so as not to teach children about VPNs — which likely **understates** VPN-specific answers.
- Ofcom: reporting bias explicitly acknowledged for the 5% VPN/circumvention figures.
- Internet Matters: sample of ~1,000 children aged 9–16, so includes under-11s and is not directly comparable with DSIT's 11–17 sample.

### Status

- Official position: circumvention of HEAA is a minority behaviour (≈5%), but circumvention of *age checks in general* is mainstream (39–53%), and displacement to unchecked sites is widespread (47% of visited porn services).
- The gap between "39% bypassed" and "5% circumvented HEAA" is the central analytical fault line. Both figures are defensible; they measure different things.

### Gaps / unknowns

- No official figure for **HEAA-specific circumvention on pornography services** as opposed to all age checks on all services.
- No longitudinal series yet: DSIT's study is a single wave (May 2026); Internet Matters' Pulse is quarterly but with a different question set; Ofcom's tracker covers July 2025–January 2026.
- No published data on **11–12s vs 16–17s specifically for pornography**, as opposed to all regulated content.
- No measurement of repeated circumvention (one-off vs habitual).
- No data on children who never encounter a check because they use platforms that have none (the 53% "platform choice" group overlaps with, but is not the same as, displacement).

## 4. Facial age estimation: error, bias and "challenge age"

Facial age estimation (FAE) is the dominant method on pornography services — **68% of all age-check completions** on Ofcom's analysed porn services between July and November 2025 (Ofcom report, para 4.13). It is accepted by Ofcom as capable of being highly effective, but its error rate is concentrated exactly at the 16–18 boundary that matters, and its fairness across demographic groups is not demonstrated in deployment.

### What we found

**Ofcom accepts FAE, with conditions**

- Source: Ofcom, *Guidance on highly effective age assurance* (April 2025), paras 4.16–4.20, 4.51, 4.22–4.37.
- URL: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/consultations/category-1-10-weeks/statement-age-assurance-and-childrens-access/part-3-guidance-on-highly-effective-age-assurance.pdf
- Key requirements: technical accuracy, robustness, reliability and fairness; a **challenge age** where age estimation is used; testing across conditions and demographic groups; mitigation of "methods of circumvention that are easily accessible to children".
- Fairness (para 4.51): the method must "avoid or minimise bias"; providers must test performance "across groups with different characteristics" and ensure it "does not produce significant bias or discriminatory outcomes" (paras 4.51, 4.70–4.72).

**Ofcom's October 2026 rapid assessment gives the error numbers**

- Source: Ofcom, *Rapid assessment of highly effective age assurance at 16*, published October 2026 (delivered to Parliament by end-October; Ofcom news item 29 October 2026).
- URL: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/online-safety/protecting-children/rapid-assessment-of-highly-effective-age-assurance-at-16.pdf
- Figures (para 3.22, using NIST FATE data):
  - For the **13–17** age group, the **lowest mean absolute error (MAE) is 1.77 years**; **19 of 50 algorithms** tested have MAE below 3 years.
  - For **18–25**, lowest MAE is **2.39 years**; **9 of 50** below 3 years.
  - "some facial age estimation technologies can accurately determine the age of users above and below 16 with a margin of error of **a few years**."
- Para 3.23: "if the facial age estimation algorithm is accurate within two years for children aged 15, it could estimate them as aged 17. This could result in **significant numbers of children under 16 gaining access**… It could also result in a 17-year-old being denied access if they are estimated to be 15."
- Para 3.27: circumvention risk from photographs, video or "any other form of spoofed representation of an older person"; liveness detection and retry limits recommended.

**Independent evidence: accuracy is worst at the boundary, and uneven across groups**

- Source: POST, *Facial age estimation* (rapid response), 16 July 2026, DOI 10.58248/RR106.
- URL: https://post.parliament.uk/facial-age-estimation/
- Quotes/findings:
  - "Home Office and NIST testing indicates FAE systems are better at judging whether someone is above or below an age threshold than estimating an exact age, with **accuracy lowest around 16 to 18 years**."
  - MAE "remains **around 2.5 years** for the 'best' systems at the critical 16 to 18 boundary according to Home Office guidance, **with consistently higher error rates for female faces**" (citing NIST IR 8525).
  - Home Office: performance can vary by "**ethnicity, skin tone, gender, place of birth and quality of input image**".
  - DSIT 2026 study: "even with 1–2 years average error, this means that there are cases where **a 12-year-old could be mistaken for 14, or a 16-year-old for 18**."
  - NIST 2024: "no single system performs consistently well across all circumstances".
  - Market failure: "the main barrier to improving accuracy and fairness was limited high-quality, representative data, and noted the market is unlikely to resolve this alone."
- Legal exposure flagged by POST: Equality Act 2010 may apply if FAE "perform[s] less well for certain groups"; ICO Children's Code expects testing, representative data and a **mechanism for users to challenge decisions**.

**Ofcom's own compliance findings: challenge age and liveness are patchy**

- Source: Ofcom report 2026.
- Pornography (para 4.11): "All but two pornography services using facial age estimation applied a challenge age approach." One of the two described its solution as a **"no threshold model"**; the other said it "does not have any predefined challenge age thresholds". Ofcom: challenge age "is key to the overall effectiveness"; where not technically feasible, providers must demonstrate an "equally effective" alternative.
- Dating (paras 6.4–6.5): two of four services had **no liveness detection**; "multiple analysed dating services deployed facial age estimation **without a challenge age**". Ofcom urged both; services have since begun introducing them.
- Cross-sector (Section 8, Figure A1): "Some services using facial age estimation have not implemented a challenge age"; "Some services that use facial age estimation or photo ID matching did not have liveness detection in place."

**Challenge age does reduce false positives — on provider-supplied data**

- Source: Ofcom rapid assessment 2026, footnote 47.
- Quote: provider-submitted performance data "indicated that **false positives rates (in this case where an individual is estimated to be over 16 when they are under 16) significantly decreased with the implementation of a challenge age** to varying degrees, depending on the challenge age set (the higher the challenge age, the fewer false positives, particularly for those age 15)."
- Caveat: this is provider-supplied, not independently verified by Ofcom.

**Ofcom acknowledges real-world defeat**

- Source: Ofcom report 2026, para 7.78.
- Quote: "BILETA raised concerns about the accuracy of facial age estimation and whether these systems can be 'defeated' by user behaviour, giving the example that the facial age estimation systems used by Reddit and Discord could reportedly be **tricked with images of an adult character from a video game**."
- Para 7.75: AVPA reported fake driving licences generated online and use of avatars from computer games; AVPA said such attacks were "typically only successful where services have disabled or weakened key safeguards".

### Status

- FAE remains an Ofcom-approved HEAA method and the workhorse of the porn sector.
- The regulator's own 2026 assessment concedes a multi-year error band at the 16/18 boundary and relies on challenge age to manage it; compliance with challenge age and liveness is incomplete.
- Fairness is a guidance criterion, not a published test standard: there is no mandatory, published, independent demographic accuracy testing regime for deployed systems.

### Gaps / unknowns

- **No UK deployment data** on FAE false positive/negative rates by skin tone, sex, age or device/camera quality. The evidence is NIST lab data, Home Office immigration-context guidance, and provider self-reports.
- No published data on how often adults are **wrongly rejected** (false negatives) and what friction/complaints that generates — relevant to the proportionality argument.
- No information on how challenge ages are actually set in the porn sector (e.g. 21, 23, 25) or whether they are audited.
- No evidence on the **retry economy**: how many attempts a child gets, whether failed attempts are logged, and whether retry limits are enforced.
- Unclear whether Ofcom has ever required a provider to change its FAE threshold or vendor as a condition of compliance (as opposed to recommending it).
- The Equality Act 2010 exposure identified by POST has not been tested in any UK enforcement or litigation.

## 5. Regulator response: guidance, assessments, enforcement

Ofcom's position has three layers: (i) HEAA guidance requiring providers to mitigate "easily accessible" circumvention; (ii) children's access assessments (s 36) as the mechanism for recording and responding to circumvention risk; (iii) enforcement — so far concentrated on services with *no* age assurance, with one significant case now testing the *quality* of assurance.

### What we found

**The core anti-circumvention duty in guidance**

- Source: Ofcom HEAA guidance, paras 4.22–4.37.
- Quote (para 4.23): "We acknowledge that it may be possible for some children to circumvent the age assurance process or access control mechanisms that the provider has put in place to meet its duties. However, risks can be mitigated by service providers taking steps to improve the robustness of their age assurance process. We therefore expect service providers to take appropriate steps to **mitigate against, and refrain from promoting, any methods of circumvention which are easily accessible to children** and where it is reasonable to assume they may use them."
- Para 4.29: providers must "identify and take appropriate steps to mitigate against methods of circumvention that are easily accessible to children".
- Para 4.30–4.33: verify **ownership** of ID, phone number, email or card (OTP/MFA); require a photo at ID upload; use liveness detection.
- Para 4.35: repeat checks "to prevent child access via **device or account sharing** and instances where children may be mistakenly classified as adults".
- Para 4.37: providers "should not publish content on their service that **directs or encourages UK users to circumvent** the age assurance process… for example by providing information about or links to a virtual private network (VPN)".

**Children's access assessments are the legal hook**

- Source: Ofcom, investigation into Aylo, 23 September 2026 (background section).
- Quote: s 36 requires "suitable and sufficient" children's access assessments, including "before making any significant change to the service's design or operation or **in response to evidence about reduced effectiveness of age assurance** that is used on the service."
- Significance: a provider that knows circumvention is happening and does not reassess is exposed under s 36 independently of s 12.

**Ofcom's public position: no silver bullet, layers required**

- Source: Ofcom, *Use of Age Assurance Report 2026* landing page, 15 July 2026 (updated 27 July 2026).
- URL: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/use-of-age-assurance-report-2026
- Quote: "**There is no single age assurance method or process that eliminates the risk of circumvention.** To make age checks more robust, there is a strong argument for **layers of protections across the system**. We expect to see further innovation from the whole tech industry — including app stores, operating systems and at device-level."
- Source: Ofcom rapid assessment, October 2026, p.3.
- Quote: "**No age assurance method is completely robust against circumvention by children.** Social media services will need to implement highly effective age assurance in full, including taking practical measures to tackle circumvention by children… However, we expect some children will attempt to get around age checks, including through the use of VPNs."

**Enforcement to date: volume, but all about absence of checks**

- Source: Ofcom press release, 15 July 2026, notes 3 and 5.
- URL: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/age-checks-helping-make-online-experiences-safer-for-uk-children-but-job-not-done-and-tech-industry-must-act-to-strengthen-protections
- Figures: **23 investigations into providers of 88 adult services**; **73%** of those services have since implemented age assurance or blocked UK users; **7 providers of 24 sites fined**: 8579 LLC (£1.35m), AVS Group Ltd (£1m), Kick Online Entertainment S.A. (£800k), provider of Fapello (£630k), Youngtek Solutions Ltd (£500k), First Time Videos LLC (£80k), Itai Tech Ltd (£50k). **Aggregate ≈ £4.41m (calculated).**
- Character of the cases: the published reasons are "not having age checks in place" and "failing to respond to a legally binding information request" — **not** circumvention failures. No published decision sanctions a provider for a circumventable check.
- Ofcom's stated prioritisation: "focusing on services who are growing their user numbers as a result of failure to put age checks in place."

**The Aylo/Pornhub case is the first real test of "highly effective" quality**

- Source: Ofcom, 23 September 2026, case CW/01358/09/26.
- Scope: s 12 (HEAA) **and** s 36 (children's access assessment). Concerns: insufficient due diligence and testing before adopting a process relying on Apple's device-level signals in May 2026.
- Why it matters for circumvention: it is the first case where the alleged failure is not "no check" but "a check that may not bind age to the user" — i.e. the identity-binding problem in §2, at ecosystem level.
- Status: open; no outcome as at 2 November 2026.

**TikTok investigation is about age *inference*, not circumvention**

- Source: Ofcom, 16 July 2026.
- URL: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/investigation-into-tiktoks-compliance-with-duties-to-protect-children-from-encountering-harmful-content-under-section-12
- Significance: Ofcom has ruled out age inference as HEAA for porn and for the under-16 ban because it operates only after a child has used the service. Relevant to circumvention only indirectly (it is a "check that arrives too late" problem).

**Ofcom is now being directed to research VPN detection**

- Source: DSIT, *Growing up in the online world: government response*, July 2026.
- Quote: "We will therefore ask Ofcom to carry out research into what additional steps services can take, to detect and mitigate circumvention, on their platforms… This will include **how services can detect VPN traffic and prevent children from using VPNs to bypass safeguards**."
- Status: not yet published as at 2 November 2026.

### Status

- The legal architecture for acting on circumvention exists (guidance + s 36 + s 12) and Ofcom has articulated it clearly.
- Enforcement has **not** yet been used against a provider for circumvention failures. Every fine to date is for missing checks or non-cooperation.
- The Aylo case is the pivot point: if Ofcom finds that relying on a third-party age signal without due diligence breaches s 12/s 36, it establishes that "someone else checked" is not a defence. If it does not, the identity-binding gap remains unenforced.

### Gaps / unknowns

- No published Ofcom guidance specific to **VPN traffic detection**, despite the July 2026 commission.
- No transparency data on how many s 36 children's access assessments Ofcom has reviewed, or how many were found inadequate.
- No enforcement outcome on **promotion of circumvention** (guidance para 4.37) — no case brought against a service for hosting "how to bypass" content, despite the July 2025 ministerial warning.
- Unclear what evidential threshold Ofcom would require to prove a provider's check was "easily circumventable" in an enforcement notice.
- No information on whether Ofcom has referred any age-assurance vendor to the ICO (a question Lord Leong could not answer in September 2025, per Mishcon).
- Ofcom's enforcement resource: ~£92m total OSA budget (Baroness Lloyd, 4 December 2025); no breakdown for age assurance/circumvention work.

## 7. Age checks defeated in practice — headline/policy level only

These are recorded for their policy significance (they show the *type* of failure and the speed of replication), not as technique documentation.

### What we found

**Video-game character's face passes a liveness check (July 2025)**

- Source: PC Gamer, 25 July 2025, "Brits can get around Discord's age verification thanks to Death Stranding's photo mode".
- URL: https://www.pcgamer.com/hardware/brits-can-get-around-discords-age-verification-thanks-to-death-strandings-photo-mode-bypassing-the-measure-introduced-with-the-uks-online-safety-act-we-tried-it-and-it-works-thanks-kojima/
- What happened (headline): within 24 hours of the duties commencing, users reported passing Discord's facial age check using a photorealistic in-game character's face, including the required mouth movement, via the game's photo mode.
- Policy significance: the failure was not the algorithm's age estimate but the **liveness/identity-binding layer**; it demonstrated that a check can be "Ofcom-approved in method" and still be defeated on day one. Ofcom later cited the Reddit/Discord game-character example in its 2026 report (para 7.78, via BILETA).

**Fake facial hair changes an age estimate (May 2026)**

- Source: *The Independent*, 1 May 2026, "Some children are drawing on fake moustaches to bypass online age checks, report finds".
- URL: https://www.the-independent.com/news/uk/home-news/children-bypassing-age-verification-social-media-b2968803.html
- Quote: a mother said, "I did catch my son using an eyebrow pencil to draw a moustache on his face, and it verified him as 15 years old."
- Policy significance: low-tech appearance manipulation shifts an estimate across a threshold; supports the argument that FAE is not robust to deliberate self-presentation, and that challenge age (not algorithm accuracy alone) is the mitigation.

**Children describing AI-generated or borrowed faces (2026)**

- Source: Internet Matters, May 2026, focus groups.
- Quotes: a 13-year-old boy: "There are lots and lots of different ways you can use AI to make your face look older." A 12-year-old girl: "I've seen clips of people online where they'll get clips of video game characters like turning their head and use it for age verification."
- Policy significance: circumvention knowledge is **peer-to-peer and video-mediated**, which is why the DSIT finding that 50% learn from friends matters more than any single technique.

**Adults using children's images to enter child-designated spaces**

- Source: Internet Matters, May 2026, p.16.
- Finding: participants had heard of adults using images or videos of children to gain access to spaces intended for younger users; the report links this to "recent media reports of adults purchasing or obtaining accounts registered to children for this purpose."
- Policy significance: this is the **reverse** identity-binding failure (adult passing as child), and it is a safeguarding risk that age assurance for porn does not address. It also supports the case for assurance at both ends of the age spectrum.

**Search-engine discoverability of unchecked sites**

- Source: Ofcom report 2026 (33% Google / 54% Bing first-page results to unprotected sites); Ofcom press release 15 July 2026.
- Policy significance: the cheapest circumvention is not defeating a check but **avoiding one** — 47% of porn services visited by children had no check. Ofcom has no HEAA power over search services (press release, note 9).

### Status

- Defeat-in-practice stories are well documented in journalism and in Ofcom's own report, but they are **anecdotal and non-representative**. No official study measures how often any given method succeeds.
- The common thread is not algorithmic sophistication but **weak binding**: liveness, ownership verification and repeat checks are the controls that fail.

### Gaps / unknowns

- No systematic tally of reported defeats, by method or by service.
- No Ofcom statement on whether the Discord/Reddit examples amounted to a breach by those services (they are user-to-user services under Part 3, not Part 5 porn providers).
- No data on how quickly services patched the reported weaknesses.
- No evidence on whether AI-generated faces are now routinely defeating commercial liveness detection in UK deployments (as opposed to being reported anecdotally).

## 8. Evidence-level tensions and gaps (not report conclusions)

1. **"Circumvention" is doing two different jobs in the debate.** Ofcom's 5% figure measures circumvention of *highly effective* checks; DSIT's 39% measures circumvention of *any* check, mostly tick boxes and date-of-birth fields. The political argument that "the regime doesn't work" largely rests on the second number; the regulatory defence rests on the first. Both are accurate.

2. **The biggest measured failure is displacement, not defeat.** 47% of porn services visited by children had no age check; search engines are outside the HEAA duty; 53% of children say they choose platforms to avoid checks. This is a system-level gap that no amount of FAE improvement closes.

3. **Identity-binding is the weak joint.** The dominant circumvention method in official data is "posing as someone else" (63% of bypassers), and the dominant facilitation is an adult (26% of parents allowed; 17% helped; 11% used a parent's ID). Ofcom's guidance names the fix (ownership verification, liveness, repeat checks) but enforcement has not yet tested it — the Aylo case is the first.

4. **Facial age estimation is accepted with a known, multi-year error band at exactly the statutory boundary.** Ofcom's own October 2026 assessment puts the best MAE at 1.77 years (13–17) and ~2.5 years at 16–18 per Home Office/NIST; challenge age is the mitigation, and compliance with challenge age and liveness is incomplete. Fairness across skin tone, sex and image quality is a guidance criterion with no published deployment testing.

5. **Policy is stable but conditional.** The Government has ruled out a VPN ban or age-gating (July 2026), while reserving the right to act if evidence worsens, and has commissioned Ofcom research on VPN detection. The Lords have twice pushed harder (Sept 2025 debate; Jan 2026 division). The conditionality is the live political risk/opportunity.

6. **The evidence base has a serious gap at the centre.** There is no official measurement of (a) how many children reach regulated porn via VPN, (b) how often HEAA specifically is defeated on porn services, or (c) the demographic error rates of deployed FAE. The three figures that would settle most of the argument do not exist.
