**Prepared:** 2 November 2026
**Purpose:** evidence gathering for a report on legal weaknesses in the UK Online Safety Act 2023 (OSA) age-verification regime. **This is not the final report.**
**Primary documents read in full:**

- *4chan Community Support LLC and Lolcow LLC v. UK Office of Communications*, No. 1:25-cv-02880 (RC) (D.D.C.), Memorandum Opinion, 15 September 2026, 24 pp., Judge Rudolph Contreras (hereafter **Opinion**).

## 0. The critical distinction: there are two events, and only one is a "ruling"

Press coverage of "the 4chan ruling" conflates two separate legal events on two continents, roughly six months apart. They must be kept apart analytically.

| | **Event A** | **Event B** |
|---|---|---|
| **What** | Ofcom confirmation decision (administrative enforcement) | US federal court judgment (Article III court) |
| **Who decided** | Ofcom (the regulator), under s. 132 OSA 2023 | Judge Rudolph Contreras, US District Court for the District of Columbia |
| **When** | Issued 19 March 2026; non-confidential version published 21 April 2026 | 15 September 2026 |
| **Against whom** | 4chan Community Support LLC only | 4chan and Lolcow LLC (Kiwi Farms) as plaintiffs; Ofcom as defendant |
| **Outcome** | Breaches of ss. 9(2), 10(5), 12 found; £520,000 fixed penalties plus daily penalties; compliance deadlines | Claim **dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction**; Ofcom immune under the FSIA |
| **Effect on the age-verification regime** | Directly imposes UK age-assurance duties on 4chan | No effect on the OSA; blocks one litigation route but leaves enforcement/collection unresolved |

**"The ruling" is Event B** — the D.D.C. judgment of 15 September 2026. Event A is a regulatory decision, not a ruling, and it is not self-executing against a US company with no UK assets.

Two widespread errors to avoid in the final report:
1. Describing the D.D.C. decision as 4chan "winning". 4chan **lost** the motion; it was dismissed from court. Its "victory" claim rests on *obiter dicta* about future enforcement (see §B.5).
2. Describing the Ofcom decision as a "court ruling" or as having been "upheld" by the US court. The US court expressly did **not** review the merits or validity of the Ofcom decision.

## A. Event A — Ofcom's decision and fines (UK, 2025–2026)

### A.1 Procedural chronology (from CD 2026 and Ofcom's case page)

- **14 April 2025** — Ofcom issues a statutory information notice under s. 100 OSA requiring 4chan's written illegal-content risk assessment record (part of the Risk Assessment Enforcement Programme). No response.
- **10 June 2025** — Ofcom opens the investigation (case ref. CW/01307/06/25).
- **16 June 2025** — Second information notice, requiring qualifying worldwide revenue ("QWR") data. No response.
- **12 August 2025** — Provisional notice of contravention under s. 130, **on the information-notice breaches only**. Threatens £20,000 plus £100/day for up to 60 days.
- **16 August 2025** — First response from 4chan's US counsel (Preston Byrne, Byrne & Storm). Asserts 4chan is an American company with "no establishment, assets, or operations in the United Kingdom"; invokes the First Amendment; says Ofcom should have used the US–UK Mutual Legal Assistance Treaty (MLAT); says US courts will not enforce foreign penalties; "does not concede jurisdiction and does not accept service" (CD 2026 §2.12).
- **27 August 2025** — 4chan and Lolcow file suit in the D.D.C. (Event B).
- **13 October 2025** — **CD 2025**: confirmation decision on the two information notices. £20,000 fixed penalty; £100/day from 14 October 2025, capped at 60 days (£6,000). Published 18 November 2025.
- **21 November 2025** — 4chan's counsel emails Ofcom: *"My client doesn't answer to you, because it is American. It did not ask to be contacted by Ofcom, it did not want to be contacted by Ofcom, Ofcom has no authority to demand anything of it"* (CD 2026 §2.15).
- **4 December 2025** — Ofcom expands the investigation to cover s. 12 (children/pornography age assurance). 4chan responds that the rules "would destroy anonymity online, which is protected by the First Amendment. Accordingly, 4chan will not be implementing your 'age assurance' rules" (CD 2026 §2.17).
- **12 February 2026** — **Second** provisional notice of contravention under s. 130, now covering ss. 9(2), 10(5) and 12. Representations due 26 February 2026.
- **27 February 2026** — 4chan acknowledges the email but makes no substantive representations.
- **19 March 2026** — **CD 2026** issued (Event A). Requirements deadline: 10 working days later, i.e. **2 April 2026**. Daily penalties run from 3 April 2026 to (at the latest) **1 June 2026**.
- **2 April 2026** — Compliance deadline passes; no compliance.
- **21 April 2026** — Non-confidential version published.
- **1 June 2026** — Outer limit of the 2026 daily penalties.
- **Early July 2026** — Payment deadline (one calendar month from the later of the decision or the last day of daily penalties) passes unpaid; 4chan confirms it will not pay.

Note: the task brief says there was a provisional notice "in 2025" covering the substantive duties. That is not quite right — the 2025 provisional notice (12 August 2025) concerned **information notices only**; the substantive ss. 9/10/12 provisional notice was **12 February 2026**.

### A.2 Findings and penalties

| Duty | Provision | Finding | Fixed penalty | Compliance deadline | Daily rate if continuing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Illegal content risk assessment | s. 9(2) | No suitable and sufficient assessment; failure **ongoing** | **£50,000** | 2 April 2026 | £200/day, max 60 days |
| Terms of service | s. 10(5) | Terms did not specify how individuals are protected from illegal content (each paragraph of s. 10(3), separating terrorism, CSEA and other priority content) | **£20,000** | 2 April 2026 | £100/day, max 60 days |
| Children / pornography | s. 12 | No highly effective age assurance; children could encounter pornography; failure **ongoing** | **£450,000** | 2 April 2026 | £500/day, max 60 days |
| Information notices (2025 decision) | s. 102(8)(a) | Failed to comply with two statutory notices (risk-assessment record; QWR) | **£20,000** | Immediate | £100/day, capped 60 days (£6,000) |

Ofcom did **not** find a breach of the general s. 10 illegal-content safety duty beyond the s. 10(5) terms-of-service duty, and did not bring separate findings on terrorism or CSEA content.

### A.3 The money — reconciling the figures

The headline "£520,000" is the sum of the three 2026 fixed penalties only. The true maximum exposure is higher:

| Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| 2026 fixed penalties (s. 12 + s. 9(2) + s. 10(5)) | £520,000 |
| 2025 fixed penalty (s. 102(8)(a)) | £20,000 |
| 2025 daily penalty (Ofcom records it as having reached the £6,000 cap) | £6,000 |
| 2026 daily penalties, if run the full 60 days (3 Apr–1 Jun 2026): £800/day aggregate | up to £48,000 |
| **Maximum aggregate** | **up to £594,000** (c. US$800,000 at c. US$1.35:£1) |

This reconciles the apparently inconsistent press figures: "£520,000" (Ofcom's headline), "£540,000" (fixed penalties across both decisions), and Dexerto's "$800k" (the fully-accrued total). Ofcom has not published a running total for 4chan, and there is no public confirmation of any payment. For the final report, the safest formulation is: **£520,000 in fixed penalties (March 2026), £20,000 in October 2025, plus daily penalties, with maximum exposure of about £594,000; nothing paid.**

Penalty-setting points worth using:
- Ofcom could not assess 4chan's turnover because 4chan refused to supply QWR: *"we have been unable to consider 4chan's financial position when determining the penalty"* (§5.48). It treated 4chan as a "small to medium sized service" and gave that factor "limited weight".
- Non-cooperation was an **aggravating** factor: correspondence was "not of a meaningful or cooperative nature" (§5.50), and Ofcom found a *"conscious and deliberate decision not to comply"* (§5.42).
- The penalties are tiny against the statutory maximum (£18m or 10% of QWR). £450,000 for the age-assurance breach is roughly 2.5% of the maximum.
- Ofcom fixed the compliance period at **10 working days**, asserting that "a wide variety of age assurance solutions ... can be implemented within 10 working days" (n.106). This is a notable proportionality point.

### A.4 Extraterritoriality reasoning

Ofcom's jurisdictional analysis is in section 4 of CD 2026.

- The Act applies to services with "links with the United Kingdom" (s. 4). Duties extend only to "the design, operation and use of the service in the UK" and, for "users", "as it affects the UK users of the service" (§3.1).
- **Significant number of UK users** (s. 4(5)(a)): 4chan's own site claims 20 million+ unique monthly visitors and that **7% of its user base is in the UK**; SimilarWeb data showed **170,000–330,000 monthly UK users** between February 2025 and February 2026. Ofcom held that "a UK user base in the hundreds of thousands is, of itself, a significant number" (§4.8).
- **Target market** (s. 4(5)(b)), in the alternative: 4chan's advertising section markets the platform on the basis that "7% of its demographic are from the UK, the second largest percentage behind the US" (§4.11). Ofcom treats advertising to UK users as evidence of targeting.
- Ofcom expressly did not decide any question of US law, saying it "would not be appropriate ... to address the claims made under US law here" (§2.13 n.8).

**Weakness signals for the report:** the "significant number" threshold is undefined in the statute and was set by Ofcom itself at "material in the nature and context of the service" rather than "large or substantial" (§4.7). The target-market finding rests substantially on a single advertising statistic. Both are administratively self-determined and were never tested on the merits in any court.

### A.5 Free expression, anonymity and proportionality

Ofcom's treatment is brief and generic — a point of vulnerability.

- The only reasoned proportionality passage is §§5.10–5.11: any interference with freedom of expression and association is "necessary and justified to secure the adequate protection of individuals in the UK from harms posed by illegal content and primary priority content that is harmful to children".
- Ofcom adds that the requirements "seek only to bring 4chan.org into compliance with the duties already enacted, and so represent interferences already decided on by Parliament", and are "the minimum necessary degree" of interference because they go "no further than the relevant duties and/or our Codes recommendations" already assessed in the Illegal Harms Statement (§5.11).
- That is a **reliance on the general, ex ante proportionality assessment**, not a 4chan-specific balancing exercise.
- **Anonymity is not analysed as a distinct issue.** Ofcom records 4chan's anonymity argument (§2.17) but does not engage with it; the word "anonymity" appears only when describing 4chan's own representations. There is no finding on whether age assurance necessarily degrades anonymity, or on privacy-preserving alternatives.
- Ofcom also did not address s. 230 of the US Communications Decency Act, the First Amendment, or the MLAT service objection on the merits.

### A.6 "Highly effective" age assurance — the four criteria

The statutory test (s. 12, read with s. 230) requires age verification or estimation "of such a kind, and used in such a way, that it is highly effective at correctly determining whether or not a particular user is a child".

Ofcom's guidance converts this into four cumulative criteria (CD 2026 §3.29):

1. **Technically accurate**
2. **Robust**
3. **Reliable**
4. **Fair**

Methods that are *not* capable of being highly effective in isolation include self-declaration, general contractual restrictions, general disclaimers, and "warnings on specific content that the content is only suitable for over 18s" (§4.54).

The factual findings against 4chan:
- A user review on **21 November 2025** found no age verification or estimation at any stage, including on adult boards; the only age reference was a Global Rule: *"You will immediately cease and not continue to access the site if you are under the age of 18"* (§4.55).
- A later review found a one-click pop-up disclaimer on Adult NSFW boards, bypassable with no age assurance, stating the content "is for mature audiences only and may not be suitable for minors" (§4.56). Ofcom held this insufficient. (The decision dates this second review "20 January 2025", which appears to be a typo for 2026, since it is described as subsequent to November 2025 — flag if quoting.)
- Pornography was found on at least 13 named boards including "Sexy Beautiful Women", "Hardcore", "Hentai", "Ecchi", "Yuri", "Yaoi", "Adult GIF" and "Adult Cartoons" (§4.52).

**Weakness signals:** the four criteria are not in the statute; they are Ofcom guidance. "Technically accurate", "robust", "reliable" and "fair" are undefined and unquantified (no error-rate thresholds, no independent testing standard). The decision contains no analysis of *which* of the four criteria 4chan's (non-)measures failed, because 4chan deployed nothing at all — so the case does not test the criteria's margins.

### A.7 4chan's position

4chan's case, as recorded in CD 2026 and its public statements, rests on five propositions:

1. **Territoriality**: 4chan is a US company with no UK establishment, assets, staff or servers; Ofcom has "no authority to demand anything of it".
2. **First Amendment**: in "the only country in which 4chan operates, the United States, it is breaking no law and indeed its conduct is expressly protected by the First Amendment" (Byrne, 19 March 2026).
3. **Anonymity**: age assurance "would destroy anonymity online, which is protected by the First Amendment".
4. **Service/international law**: Ofcom bypassed the US–UK MLAT; the notices are therefore ineffective in the US.
5. **Non-enforceability**: US courts will not enforce foreign regulatory penalties.

It is important for the report that propositions 2–4 were **never adjudicated**. The D.D.C. dismissed on jurisdiction without reaching them, and no appeal by 4chan to the Upper Tribunal (s. 168 OSA) is publicly recorded, nor did 4chan make substantive representations in the Ofcom process.

- On the March 2026 decision: *"Mr Whiskers looks forward to your confirmation decision, which will be shredded and used to line his enclosure upon receipt."* (attributed to Byrne by *The Independent*, 10 July 2026.)

## B. Event B — the US court judgment (D.D.C., 15 September 2026)

### B.1 Case and procedural posture

- **Case:** *4chan Community Support LLC and Lolcow LLC v. UK Office of Communications*, No. 1:25-cv-02880 (RC), US District Court for the District of Columbia.
- **Judge:** Rudolph Contreras.
- **Filed:** 27 August 2025 (complaint). Ofcom moved to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction on **1 December 2025**; opposition **29 December 2025**; reply **16 January 2026**.
- **Decided:** 15 September 2026 (Memorandum Opinion and separate Order). 24 pages.
- **Claims:** violations of the First, Fourth and Fifth Amendments; conflict with s. 230 of the Communications Decency Act; due process; repugnancy to US public policy; improper service (MLAT). Relief sought: declaratory judgment and an injunction restraining Ofcom from enforcing the OSA against them in the US.
- **Defence:** foreign sovereign immunity under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), 28 U.S.C. §§ 1602–1611; alternatively lack of standing (Lolcow), *forum non conveniens* and international comity.

### B.2 Holding

The court **granted Ofcom's motion and dismissed the complaint for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction**. It addressed only the immunity argument and expressly did not reach standing, *forum non conveniens* or comity (Opinion at 8: "The Court addresses only Ofcom's first argument").

Two independent holdings:

1. **Ofcom is a "foreign state" within s. 1603(a) FSIA.** Applying the D.C. Circuit's "categorical approach", the court held Ofcom's "core functions" are governmental, not commercial. It rejected the argument that Ofcom's statutory independence and separate legal personality made it a private corporation, finding it had "demonstrated that it bears a sufficient connection to the British government" (Opinion at 13).
2. **The commercial-activity exception (s. 1605(a)(2)) does not apply.** The "gravamen" of the complaint is regulatory coercion, not commerce.

### B.3 Reasoning on "foreign state"

The court's core reasoning (Opinion at 13):

> "Ofcom's core functions involve the regulation of telecommunications. ... By statute, its 'principal duty' is 'to further the interests of [British] citizens in relation to communications matters.' ... The Online Safety Act further authorizes Ofcom to issue certain regulations, conduct investigations, and bring enforcement actions against regulated entities—all backed by the threat of fines and imprisonment."

> "These are quintessential government functions. Ofcom creates legal rules that bind other parties (regulations), investigates violations of law (information notices), and enforces the law by [penalties]."

Procedural point: the court **also held that the plaintiffs had forfeited** any argument that Ofcom is not a foreign state, because they merely "disagree[d] with th[e] premise" and incorporated the complaint by reference without developing the point (Opinion at 11–12). The merits holding is therefore alternative ("In any event, the Court also agrees with Ofcom...").

### B.4 Reasoning on the commercial-activity exception

The court distinguished commercial from regulatory activity, quoting *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*: the exception does not apply when the state acts "as a regulator of a market" rather than "in the manner of a private player within" it.

> "Plaintiffs allege that Ofcom is investigating, and perhaps prosecuting, violations of British law. **That is classic regulator, and thus sovereign, behavior.**" (Opinion at 16.)

> "Like the bank in *Rosenkrantz*, Ofcom is threatening to impose sanctions on a variety of private actors with whom it lacks a contractual or other voluntary relationship. **Its only relationship with Plaintiffs is that of a regulator to a regulated party.** ... [F]ines and imprisonment are generally sanctions that only sovereigns may levy." (Opinion at 19.)

The court rejected the argument that Ofcom's conduct must be commercial because a US regulator could not constitutionally do the same:

> "Even without deciding whether Ofcom's conduct would be unlawful coming from an American regulator, Plaintiffs' argument suffers two fatal flaws. First, nothing in the case law that Plaintiffs cite holds that the distinction between regulatory and commercial activity turns on what an American sovereign is empowered to do. On the contrary, the Supreme Court has held that activity which amounts to an 'abuse of [ ] power,' and would presumably violate the United States Constitution, still constitutes sovereign activity 'however monstrous such abuse undoubtedly may be.'" (Opinion at 17, quoting *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*.)

And, on compelled versus voluntary moderation:

> "Voluntarily adopting content-moderation standards is not the same as being compelled to do so by a government regulator—often backed, as in this case, by the threat of fines or imprisonment. It is the difference between gifting someone $50 and being mugged with $50 in your wallet." (Opinion at 18.)

The court also rejected the GARM analogy (private boycotts/coordination) as "different in kind from directly imposing regulations with the threat of fines and prison", and refused jurisdictional discovery.

### B.5 The enforcement *dicta* — the part 4chan calls its victory

Having dismissed the case, the court volunteered guidance on what would happen if Ofcom tried to collect in the US (Opinion at 22–23):

> "Second, dismissing Plaintiffs' suit here hardly leaves Plaintiffs defenseless. To start, Plaintiffs may defend themselves in the British courts and avail themselves of the rights afforded to them there. ... But even without that, if Ofcom obtains a foreign judgment against Plaintiffs, Ofcom will still need to enforce that judgment. Because Plaintiffs represent that they have 'no presence, infrastructure, or operations abroad,' that likely means enforcing any judgment in the United States. At that point, Plaintiffs could avail themselves of the protection of American courts. **American courts generally decline to enforce foreign judgments that violate the United States Constitution**, if indeed Plaintiffs are correct in their constitutional claims." (citing *Matusevitch v. Telnikoff*.)

> "Congress has even spoken directly to Plaintiffs' concerns. It enacted the SPEECH Act to prevent American courts from 'recogniz[ing] or enforc[ing] a foreign judgment for defamation' unless the judgment is consistent with the First Amendment."

> "Finally, **at the risk of gilding the lily**, to the extent any foreign judgment against Plaintiffs were penal in nature, Plaintiffs may find a receptive audience before American courts regardless of the judgment's compatibility with the United States Constitution. That is because **American courts are not necessarily required to enforce foreign penal judgments.**" (citing *Huntington v. Attrill*; *Yahoo! Inc. v. LICRA*; Restatement (Third) of Foreign Relations Law § 483.)

And the closing line (Opinion at 23–24):

> "Put simply, Plaintiffs' suit fails because they seek to use American courts as **a sword against Ofcom rather than as a shield to protect themselves.**"

### B.6 What the court did NOT decide

This is essential for the report; several commentators overstate the judgment.

- **It did not decide the First Amendment merits.** It assumed, without deciding, that Ofcom's conduct might be unconstitutional if done by a US regulator ("Even without deciding whether Ofcom's conduct would be unlawful coming from an American regulator...").
- **It did not decide whether the OSA is unconstitutional as applied to 4chan**, nor whether age verification violates the First Amendment or anonymity rights.
- **It did not decide whether Ofcom's penalties are enforceable in the US.** The enforceability discussion is *obiter dicta*; the court had already held it lacked jurisdiction.
- **It did not decide Lolcow's standing**, *forum non conveniens*, or comity.
- **It did not hold that Ofcom's fines are "void"** — that is Byrne & Storm's characterisation, not the court's holding.
- It did not address s. 230, the Fourth Amendment, due process, or the MLAT service objection.

### B.7 Appeal, subsequent filings, enforcement action

- **No appeal on the docket** as of the last public docket update (CourtListener, 17 October 2026). The ordinary federal appeal window is **30 days** under FRAP 4(a)(1)(A), which would have expired around **15 October 2026**; the 60-day rule in FRAP 4(a)(1)(B) applies only where the United States is a party, and Ofcom is a foreign sovereign, so it should not apply. On that basis the time to appeal appears to have expired without a notice of appeal. **Verify the docket before publication**, because if a 60-day window were somehow held to apply it would run to mid-November 2026.
- Byrne & Storm said on 16 September 2026 that they were *"reviewing the decision and considering all options, including appeal"* (Reclaim The Net). No appeal has been announced since.
- Byrne's later public position (20 October 2026) is that the case is over "for all practical purposes" and that an Ofcom enforcement action in the US would be "suicidal" for Ofcom. This suggests no appeal is intended.
- **No public record of Ofcom applying for a business disruption order / access restriction order against 4chan** as at 2 November 2026. Ofcom's own position (20 July 2026 explainer) is that it cannot apply to block a site **for non-payment alone**; it needs continuing non-compliance with a duty, and even then the furthest a UK court could go is a UK geo-block, not a global shutdown.
- **The fines remain unpaid.** Ofcom's evidence to the DCMS Committee on 20 October 2026: 11 OSA fines issued since March 2025, **2 paid, 9 unpaid** (1 under appeal, 1 not yet overdue, 7 overdue). 4chan is one of the nine.

### B.8 Practical implications

1. **Ofcom must enforce in the US, or not at all.** 4chan has no UK assets; the court itself says enforcement "likely means enforcing any judgment in the United States".
2. **Enforcement is legally hazardous for Ofcom.** To collect, Ofcom would likely have to submit to US jurisdiction, at which point 4chan could raise the constitutional defences that the D.D.C. never reached. The court's dicta signal a "chilly reception".
3. **The judgment creates no binding precedent.** It is a district court decision; persuasive in the D.D.C., not binding on other circuits.
4. **The OSA's coercive machinery is untouched.** The Act, the age-assurance duties and Ofcom's powers remain in force. What is exposed is the *collection* layer, not the *obligation* layer.
5. **The realistic escalation is infrastructure-level, not financial**: business disruption measures (payment/advertising providers, ISP blocking) under ss. 145–146 OSA, and Ofcom's request for interim business disruption powers. Both are UK-territorial and VPN-circumventable.

### C.2 Ofcom and UK government statements

- **Suzanne Cater, Director of Enforcement, Ofcom** (19 March 2026): *"Companies – wherever they're based – are not allowed to sell unsafe toys to children in the UK. And society has long protected youngsters from things like alcohol, smoking and gambling. The digital world should be no different. ... Age checks and risk assessments are cornerstones of our laws, and we'll take robust enforcement action against firms that fall short."*
- **Ofcom spokesperson** (16 September 2026, to Reuters): *"A U.S. Court has acknowledged that we are within our rights as a UK regulator to investigate breaches of the UK's online safety laws, and has dismissed claims by 4chan and Kiwi Farms who have sought to stop us doing our job of creating a safer life online for people in the UK."*
- **Ofcom** (20 July 2026 explainer): *"If a company fails to pay a fine, we will pursue recovery of that debt wherever possible, regardless of where the firm is based."* But: *"we do not have the legal powers to apply to block a site in the UK for failure to pay a fine alone."*
- **UK Government spokesperson** (to *The Independent*, July 2026): *"Platforms have a legal duty to protect UK users under the OSA, regardless of where they are based. ... Ofcom has our full backing to act."*

### C.3 4chan / Byrne & Storm

- **Preston Byrne** (19 March 2026): *"In the only country in which 4chan operates, the United States, it is breaking no law and indeed its conduct is expressly protected by the First Amendment."*
- **Byrne** (July 2026): *"My client's position is that the OSA's extraterritorial enforcement against it is theatre. We are confused by Ofcom's persistence in pursuing American targets who are under no obligation to pay any fine to anyone, and engaged in perfectly lawful, and indeed constitutionally protected, activity here in the United States."*
- **Byrne & Storm** (16 September 2026): *"To be clear: Ofcom will never collect a penny of the fines it purported to impose on 4chan, or compel 4chan to obey its orders. Those fines and orders are void in the United States."* And: *"Under the rule announced yesterday, a foreign censor enjoys greater protection in an American courtroom than the American citizens it threatens with fines and arrest. ... This situation is lawless, and only Congress can fix it."*
- **Byrne** (20 October 2026): *"Insofar as a political organization like Ofcom is even capable of admitting defeat, it did so in the 4chan enforcement today."*

### C.4 UK parliamentary comment

**(a) DCMS Committee, 20 October 2026 — Dame Melanie Dawes (Ofcom chief executive)**

- 11 OSA fines since March 2025; 2 paid; 9 unpaid (1 under appeal, 1 not yet overdue, 7 overdue).
- Of the unpaid cases: 2 services introduced effective age assurance, 4 geo-blocked the UK, 3 remain accessible with risks unmitigated.
- *"We won't be able to recover every single fine on online safety. If foreign courts decide to try and get in our way, there will be a point at which we have to accept that we won't be able to recover that money."*
- On territorial limits: *"Our reading of the Act is that we do not have any capacity to require a website, for example, to block themselves in the United States ... The Americans are pretty clear about that as well ... the furthest a UK court could go ... is to secure a geo-block ... but it is as far as you can go."*
- Dawes also flagged that Ofcom wants **interim business disruption measures** (pre-investigation blocking powers).

**(b) Westminster Hall debate, 29 October 2026 (Hansard, Vol. 792)**

- **Rebecca Paul MP (Reigate, Con)**: *"Giving evidence to the Digital, Culture, Media and Sport Committee earlier this month, Ofcom's chief executive said that only two out of 11 online safety fines had been paid. In the case of 4chan, it was fined £520,000 in March for breaches, including failing to introduce effective age checks to stop children accessing pornography. Its US lawyer responded to demands for payment with an image of an AI-generated hamster. Unfortunately, that example shows how difficult it can be to enforce against overseas entities, putting Ofcom in an extremely difficult position. I would be grateful if the Minister gave her views on the type of enforcement action that should be taken in these difficult cases, such as last-resort business disruption powers."*
- **Vikki Slade MP (Lib Dem)**: *"If nine out of 11 fines remain unpaid, that raises serious questions about Ofcom's effectiveness and suggests that fines are not acting as a meaningful deterrent."*
- **Stephanie Peacock MP, Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State for DCMS** (reply): did not address 4chan directly; said the Government has asked Ofcom to publish an enforcement strategy and that *"Sir Ian Cheshire, the Ofcom chair, is reviewing Ofcom's enforcement capability, and we will consider his findings carefully. If more changes are needed, we will not hesitate to act."*
- No minister has defended the fines' collectability or announced a specific enforcement plan for 4chan.

### C.5 US legislative response

- **GRANITE Act** — H.R. 10174 (introduced 27 August 2026, Rep. Michael Rulli); Senate companion S. 5478. Would strip sovereign immunity from foreign regulators accused of censoring Americans and create a civil cause of action.
- **HOMEFRONT Act** — H.R. 10309 (introduced 8 September 2026, Rep. Warren Davidson). Related foreign-censorship shield.
- Byrne's public campaign is explicitly built around the 4chan case: *"Ofcom conceding defeat over 4chan because it fears American courts is the entire case for the GRANITE Act."*
- As at 2 November 2026 these are introduced bills, not law.

### C.6 Academic and legal commentary

- **James Martin, "Law Without Borders: Ofcom vs. 4chan and The Next Great Internet Speech Fight", Suffolk Journal of High Technology Law (19 Nov 2025)** — pre-ruling student commentary; correctly predicted dismissal on personal jurisdiction, FSIA and/or comity grounds; notes the case's significance for the First Amendment and cross-border platform regulation. URL: sites.suffolk.edu/jhtl/2025/11/19/law-without-borders-ofcom-vs-4chan-and-the-next-great-internet-speech-fight.
- **Terry Green & Amélie Hitchings, Katten Muchin Rosenman, "'Dear Ofcom, your case is legally void and will make excellent hamster bedding'" (5 Feb 2026)** — best pre-ruling legal chronology; identifies the four claimed grounds (improper service/MLAT, First/Fourth/Fifth Amendments, s. 230, commercial-activity exception) and Ofcom's six counter-arguments. URL: quickreads.ext.katten.com/post/102mgpt/...
- **Terry Green & Larry Wong, "Lessons Learnt from Ofcom's First Enforcement of the Online Safety Act — Lessons from 4chan" (Katten, 2025)** — procedural lessons on information notices, service and extraterritoriality.

- **Global Freedom of Expression (Columbia), case note (2026)** — treats the Ofcom decision as the "decision" and analyses it under ECHR Article 10; useful on proportionality but contains a minor date error (describes the "final decision" as 21 April 2026, which is the publication date, not the issue date of 19 March 2026).
- No peer-reviewed article on the D.D.C. judgment has been located (expected — it is seven weeks old). No law-review treatment of the FSIA/OSA interface yet.

### C.7 Kiwi Farms / Lolcow parallel track

- Lolcow LLC (West Virginia) operates Kiwi Farms. It was a co-plaintiff in the D.D.C. case but **was never the subject of an Ofcom information notice, investigation or enforcement proceeding**.
- Ofcom's evidence: it sent Lolcow a March 2025 advisory letter; after Lolcow **temporarily geo-blocked the UK**, Ofcom "decided not to proceed with issuing an information notice", reserving the right to revisit (Opinion at 6–8).
- Lolcow later restored access without the UK IP block, in what it described as an obligation "to do its part to defend the First Amendment, and resist" (Opinion at 7).
- Its position in the litigation was that Ofcom's letter and threatened penalties violated US law; the court did not reach Lolcow's standing because it dismissed on immunity.
- **No separate Kiwi Farms enforcement proceeding exists.** The "parallel proceeding" is the same D.D.C. case, not a separate one. Any suggestion that Kiwi Farms was fined is wrong.

## F. Evidence gaps, discrepancies and cautions

1. **£520,000 vs £540,000 vs £546,000 vs £594,000.** Use the breakdown in §A.3. Do not write "£520,000 total" without qualification; it excludes the 2025 penalty and all daily penalties.
2. **"9 of 11 fines unpaid"** is correct for 20 October 2026, but only **7 were overdue**; one was under appeal and one not yet due. Precision matters.
3. **Ofcom's own fine counts conflict.** Its 19 March 2026 press release says "16 fines ... against six companies, totalling nearly £4 million"; Dawes told MPs "11 fines" on 20 October 2026. The difference may be counting decisions vs individual penalty notices. Flag rather than reconcile.
4. **The Ofcom decision is not a "ruling" and has not been judicially reviewed.** No appeal by 4chan to the Upper Tribunal under s. 168 is publicly recorded. No UK court has tested ss. 4, 9, 10 or 12 as applied to 4chan.
5. **The D.D.C. judgment decided jurisdiction only.** Any statement that the court "ruled the fines unenforceable", "struck down the OSA", or "vindicated the First Amendment" is wrong.
6. **The foreign-state holding rests partly on waiver.** The court first held the argument forfeited, then held alternatively on the merits.
7. **"Ofcom is a foreign state" vs "instrumentality".** The court held Ofcom is a foreign state actor under the categorical approach, not merely an agency/instrumentality. Some coverage (ICLG) describes it as "sufficient connection"; both are accurate but the doctrinal route differs.
8. **The "20 January 2025" date at CD 2026 §4.56 appears to be a typo** for 2026 (it describes a review subsequent to 21 November 2025). Avoid quoting that date.
9. **Hamster quotes vary between sources.** The Katten and *Independent* versions differ; the emails are not in the published decision (annexes redacted).
10. **No appeal has been filed** as at 2 November 2026, but the window had not closed. Check the docket before publication.
11. **No business disruption proceedings** against 4chan are publicly recorded. Do not assert that Ofcom has applied for a block.
12. **Kiwi Farms was not fined and no separate proceeding exists.** It was a co-plaintiff only.
13. **Gadget Review and LegalTech Digest coverage could not be verified.** Do not cite without a direct check.
