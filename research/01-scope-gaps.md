# Dossier 01 — Statutory scope and definition gaps in the UK Online Safety Act 2023 age-verification regime for pornography

**Prepared:** 2 November 2026
**Purpose:** Evidence gathering for a report on legal weaknesses/vulnerabilities in Part 5 (ss 79–82) of the Online Safety Act 2023 ("OSA"). This is **not** the final report.
**Method:** Primary legislation; Ofcom statutory guidance, statements and enforcement notices; parliamentary and government material; regulator-commissioned research; academic commentary; legal/professional blogs; NGO and news reporting. Searches were run on 2 November 2026. The web-search integration failed intermittently during the session; where a search engine was unavailable, sources were retrieved directly from known URLs and PDFs. This is noted where it affects coverage.

**Confidence key used below**

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Confirmed** | Clear in the statutory text, a regulator statement, or a court/regulator document. |
| **Alleged** | Asserted by a named actor (NGO, journalist, industry body) but not established by a regulator or court. |
| **Contested** | There are credible arguments or evidence on both sides; no authoritative resolution found. |
| **Speculation / analytical** | A structurally plausible gap identified by this dossier or by commentary, but no direct documentary evidence of use. |

## 1. "Significant number of UK users" and "target market" are undefined; providers self-assess scope

**Description:** Section 80(4) gives Part 5 extra-territorial reach where a service has "a significant number of United Kingdom users" or where "United Kingdom users form one of the target markets". Neither expression is defined, no numeric threshold is set, and the provider makes the initial judgement. A service with a small but real UK audience can plausibly conclude it is out of scope.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 80(4):** "A service 'has links with the United Kingdom' for the purposes of this Part if either of the following conditions is met in relation to the service— (a) the service has a significant number of United Kingdom users, or (b) United Kingdom users form one of the target markets for the service (or the only target market)." No definition follows. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/80> (confirmed).
- **Ofcom, Guidance on highly effective age assurance and other Part 5 duties, 16 January 2025, para 3.30:** "The Act does not define what is meant by a 'significant number' of UK users for the purposes of considering the 'UK links' condition for the purposes of Part 5. Service providers should be able to explain their judgement, especially if they think they do not have a significant number of UK users." <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/consultations/category-1-10-weeks/statement-age-assurance-and-childrens-access/guidance-on-highly-effective-age-assurance-and-other-part-5-duties.pdf?v=388810> (confirmed).
- **Same guidance, para 3.31:** "We consider that the concept of 'significant number of UK users' should be understood as meaning that the number of UK users on the service is material in the nature and context of the service in question, rather than the number of UK users of the service necessarily being a large or substantial number. We do not consider that the UK links test is intended to exclude services from the scope of the Part 5 duties simply because they have relatively small user bases … We therefore suggest service providers err on the side of caution when assessing whether they have a significant number of UK users." (confirmed).
- **Ofcom, Statement: Age Assurance and Children's Access, 16 January 2025, para 4.52:** repeats that the test is not intended to exclude small user bases and says providers should "err on the side of caution". Crucially, Ofcom still gives no number or safe harbour. <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/consultations/category-1-10-weeks/statement-age-assurance-and-childrens-access/statement-age-assurance-and-childrens-access.pdf?v=397036> (confirmed).
- **Ofcom guidance, para 3.34:** lists non-exhaustive "target market" indicia: designed for UK users; promoted or marketed to UK users; generates revenue from UK users directly or indirectly (including advertising); functionalities or content tailored for UK users; UK domain or UK contact address/telephone number. This is guidance, not a test. (confirmed).
- **Counter-evidence — Open Rights Group, Online Safety Act: A Guide for Organisations Working with the Act, 15 May 2025:** states that "even small niche providers of pornographic content are required to implement HEAA measures", because there is "no minimum number of users required for the provisions of Part 5 to bite". <https://www.openrightsgroup.org/publications/online-safety-act-a-guide-for-organisations-working-with-the-act/> (alleged/advocacy; it overstates the certainty of the statutory position).
- **Structural context — DSIT, Online Safety Act enactment impact assessment, 23 October 2024, para 115:** an assessment of the top 200 pornography sites found "36% of sites (or 72 sites) and 16% of traffic was to sites outside of the scope of the OSA's core duties". This shows a large long tail that depends on the Part 5 route (or on no route at all). <https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6716222b9242eecc6c849b09/Online_Safety_act_enactment_impact_assessment.pdf> (confirmed official estimate).

**Status:** The statutory indeterminacy is **confirmed**. The claim that providers exploit it to escape scope is **speculation/analytical** — no published Ofcom decision, enforcement notice, or provider statement was found in which a service successfully relied on "not a significant number" or "not a target market". Ofcom's own stance cuts against a narrow reading, but it is unenforceable as a threshold.

**Gaps/unknowns:** No numeric threshold or safe harbour; no published Ofcom decision quantifying "significant"; no data on how many long-tail sites self-assess out; no evidence on whether ad-funded sites with low UK traffic are treated as "targeting" the UK; no litigation construing s 80(4) was found.

## 2. Part 5 lacks the Part 3 section 4(6) "capable of being used in the UK + material risk of significant harm" limb

**Description:** Part 3 services can be in scope on a second, risk-based route: capable of being used in the UK plus reasonable grounds for believing there is a material risk of significant harm. Part 5 has no equivalent. A provider-pornography service with a very small UK audience that does not "target" the UK can therefore escape Part 5 even if it is accessible and harmful.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 4(5)–(6):** s 4(5) is the "significant number"/"target market" test; s 4(6) adds: "the service is capable of being used in the United Kingdom by individuals, and there are reasonable grounds to believe that there is a material risk of significant harm to individuals in the United Kingdom presented by … user-generated content … or search content". <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/4> (confirmed).
- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 80(4):** contains only the two s 4(5)-type limbs; there is no "capable of being used"/"material risk" limb for Part 5. (confirmed).
- **Bratby Law, "Does the Online Safety Act apply to my service?", 29 August 2026:** "For a Part 5 pornography service the UK-links test is section 80(4), which carries the section 4(5) limbs only." The article also notes that s 4(6) can catch a service with UK users who are not "significant in number". <https://bratby.law/does-the-online-safety-act-apply/> (confirmed legal analysis).
- **Alexandros Antoniou, "Bringing small high-harm platforms into the online safety regime: how one word changed the game", Online Safety Act Network, 19 October 2023:** explains that the risk-based limb was the mechanism for bringing small high-harm services into the regime, and that size-based thresholds alone left "a large gap in a risk-based regime". <https://www.onlinesafetyact.net/analysis/bringing-small-high-harm-platforms-into-the-online-safety-regime-how-one-word-changed-the-game/> (confirmed/analytical; the piece is about Part 3 categorisation, but the policy point transfers).
- **McGlynn, Woods & Antoniou, "Pornography, the Online Safety Act 2023 and the Need for Further Reform", Journal of Media Law 16(2) (2024) 211–239:** describes the OSA's links test as covering services with a significant number of UK users, UK target market, "or if the service is accessible in the UK and there is a material risk of significant harm", and says this brings in "small non-UK based 'free speech' platforms". The article does not separately analyse the Part 5 omission. DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1080/17577632.2024.2357421>; open copy: <https://repository.essex.ac.uk/38455/1/Pornography%20%20the%20Online%20Safety%20Act%202023%20and%20the%20need%20for%20further%20reform.pdf> (confirmed).

**Status:** The statutory asymmetry is **confirmed**. Whether it matters in practice is **contested**: most pure provider-porn sites with any meaningful UK traffic will be caught by "target market" or "significant number". The gap bites hardest on low-reach, high-harm, offshore sites that do not market to the UK.

**Gaps/unknowns:** No litigation or Ofcom decision on the omission was found; no evidence of a specific service relying on it; whether Ofcom could use Part 3 duties instead where the service also has U2U or search functionality; whether "capable of being used in the UK" would have been constitutionally or practically objectionable for Part 5.

## 3. The UGC carve-out in s 79(7): tube, cam and fan sites fall under Part 3, not Part 5 — and Pornhub reportedly used this to delay checks

**Description:** "Provider pornographic content" excludes user-generated content (s 79(7)). Large tube, cam and creator-platform sites are therefore regulated principally as user-to-user services under Part 3, whose children's duties began on 25 July 2025 rather than Part 5's 17 January 2025. The Christian Institute and the Age Verification Providers Association accused Pornhub of relying on this distinction to delay robust checks.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 79(7):** "Pornographic content that is user-generated content in relation to an internet service is not to be regarded as provider pornographic content in relation to that service." <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/79> (confirmed).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, paras 3.11–3.13 and 3.25–3.26:** expressly lists "Pornographic content that is user-generated content" as outside Part 5 and says such content is regulated under Part 3; a predominantly U2U service is subject to Part 5 only for the pornography "that the provider itself (or a person acting on behalf of the provider) publishes or displays". (confirmed).
- **The Christian Institute, "Pornhub 'exploits' loophole to delay protecting kids", 29 November 2024:** "Pornhub claims it is exempt from Ofcom requirements for 'providers of pornographic content published or displayed on the service', which come into force in January … Instead, the website is trying to argue that it will be covered by similar rules for user-generated content, which are not expected to be introduced until July." The Age Verification Providers Association is quoted: "To the general public, such sites would often be the first they think of when they consider pornographic sites, and are not obviously user-to-user services." Its Executive Director Iain Corby: "We expect Ofcom to produce unambiguous guidance that establishes a level playing field where all porn sites must require highly effective age assurance simultaneously." <https://www.christian.org.uk/news/pornhub-exploits-loophole-to-delay-protecting-kids/> (alleged/reported).
- **The Telegraph, "Pornhub to dodge age verification checks", 26 November 2024:** the Christian Institute links to this as the origin of the story. Headline and URL indicate the same allegation. <https://www.telegraph.co.uk/business/2024/11/26/pornhub-dodge-age-verification-not-providing-pornography/> (alleged; paywalled, headline/URL verified only).
- **The Christian Institute, "Ofcom: 'Access to online porn must be ring-fenced against under-18s'", 24 January 2025:** repeats that Pornhub "claimed that it is exempt" and was "trying to argue that it will be covered by similar rules for user-generated content, which are not to be enforced until July". It contrasts Ofcom's position that "Services which host their own pornography must start to introduce age checks immediately, while other user-to-user services – including social media – which allow pornography … will have to follow suit by July at the latest." <https://www.christian.org.uk/news/ofcom-access-to-online-porn-must-be-ring-fenced-against-under-18s/> (alleged/reported; the Ofcom quote is confirmed).
- **Handley Gill, "Hubbub?", 25 September 2026:** by 2026 Pornhub/Aylo is regulated under **both** Part 5 and Part 3; Ofcom's September 2026 investigation is under Part 3 (s 36 children's access assessment and s 12 age assurance), not Part 5. This indicates that the U2U route is now the operative route for Pornhub's own compliance. <https://www.handleygill.co.uk/handley-gill-blog/online-safety-act-2023-highly-effective-age-assurance-ofcom-pornhub-investigation-announced> (confirmed legal analysis; the underlying Ofcom investigation is confirmed, the "both routes" characterisation is the author's).
- **BBFC/Ofcom research, Functionality of Online Pornography Services (2023):** 47% of the top 100 UK porn services were "tube services"; 14% cam services; 18% aggregators. These are the categories most likely to be U2U and therefore outside Part 5 for most of their content. <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/online-research/bbfc-research/functionality-of-online-pornography-services-report> (confirmed).

**Status:** The statutory carve-out is **confirmed**. The allegation that Pornhub relied on it to delay checks is **alleged/reported** by the Christian Institute and the Telegraph; no direct Aylo statement or Ofcom correspondence was found. The practical effect — a six-month gap between Part 5 (17 January 2025) and the Part 3 children's duties (25 July 2025) — is **confirmed**.

**Gaps/unknowns:** No direct quote from Aylo/Pornhub; no published Ofcom rejection of the argument; no enforcement action specifically for the January–July 2025 period found; whether Ofcom's guidance actually resolved the ambiguity before 17 January 2025; current compliance of tube/cam sites under Part 3 (Ofcom's 2026 report says 64 of the top 100 porn services had age assurance by June 2026, with 10 geo-blocking).

## 4. The provider/UGC boundary: creator revenue-share, content-partner programmes and embedded UGC

**Description:** Whether content is "provider" or "user-generated" determines whether Part 5's January 2025 duty or Part 3's July 2025 duty applies. Ofcom/BBFC research found the boundary often impossible to draw from outside, especially where creators are on revenue-share or "content partner" deals, or where a tube site and a production company share a parent.

**What we found**

- **BBFC/Ofcom, Functionality of Online Pornography Services (2023), p 5:** "We could not always differentiate between UGC and provider content. Some services may be carrying both UGC and provider content, but this was often not obvious." (confirmed, regulator-commissioned research).
- **Same report, p 5:** "The implications of commercial arrangements between individual creators or pornography production companies and services are not clear. The commercial arrangements … (such as a shared parent company, or content partner program) may have an impact on whether content uploaded to a service by a creator or production company should be considered UGC or provider content. This may merit further analysis. Furthermore, it is often difficult to determine where these arrangements are in place, because the information is typically not public-facing." (confirmed).
- **Same report, p 3:** 24% of services offered a "content partner program" or similar; 39% hosted only UGC; 26% hosted only provider content; 4% hosted both; **31% hosted UGC but the researchers could not determine whether provider content was also present**. (confirmed).
- **Same report, p 17:** channels (branded production-company pages) were present on 31% of services, 94% of them tube services. "In some cases, the production company and the service may be owned by the same parent company, which potentially suggests a commercial relationship that is distinct from the relationship between the service and a typical content creator. This may have implications for whether such content is considered UGC or provider content." (confirmed).
- **Same report, p 18:** "If channels are taken to be indicative of provider content, then the percentage of services publishing provider content would more than double, rising from 30% … to 61%." (confirmed).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, paras 3.14–3.16:** the provider is "the entity or individual that has control over which content is published or displayed"; Ofcom is "likely to consider a service provider to have exercised control … where it exercises editorial control over the nature, selection or presentation of the content". Examples given are a creator uploading to a service they control, or a studio publishing to a service it controls. (confirmed).
- **Embedded content:** s 79(6)(a)(ii) says "published or displayed" includes "pornographic content that is embedded on the service". But s 79(7) says UGC is not provider content. The interaction between an embedded third-party player and the UGC exclusion is not addressed in the guidance. (confirmed text; application uncertain).

**Status:** The factual indeterminacy is **confirmed** by Ofcom's own research. The legal effect is **contested/unresolved**: no Ofcom decision applying "control" to a content-partner programme, revenue-share arrangement, or shared-parent-company structure was found. The 30% vs 61% range is the strongest quantitative evidence that the boundary is doing real work.

**Gaps/unknowns:** No case on revenue-share was found; no guidance on what level of revenue share or editorial curation turns UGC into provider content; no treatment of embedded UGC players; no evidence on whether services have restructured contracts to stay on the Part 3 side of the line; Ofcom has not published a list of services it treats as Part 5 vs Part 3.

## 5. Search-results exclusion (s 79(6)(b)) and the absence of a search-specific HEAA duty

**Description:** Part 5 expressly does not apply to pornographic content appearing in search results. Search services have Part 3 duties, but no duty to use highly effective age assurance to stop children reaching pornographic sites. Ofcom's July 2026 report found a third of Google first-page results and over half of Bing first-page results for relevant searches pointed to unprotected porn sites, and that 62% of children who visited porn used a search engine immediately beforehand.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 79(6)(b):** references to pornographic content "published or displayed" on a service "do not include references to pornographic content that appears in search results of a search service or a combined service". (confirmed).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, para 3.17:** "Pornographic content which appears in the search results of a search engine or a combined service is not treated as published or displayed on the search service or combined service in question for the purposes of Part 5 … These services will be subject to obligations in Part 3 of the Act, including the children's risk assessment and safety duties in sections 28 to 30 (and for a combined service also in sections 11 to 13)." (confirmed).
- **Ofcom, "Age checks helping make online experiences safer for UK children…", 15 July 2026:** "We found that 33% of results returned on the first page of Google Search and 54% on Bing were to pornography sites without age checks or other protections." Note 6: "Sixty-two per cent of children who visited pornographic services in our study used a search engine immediately before visiting pornography services, at least once." Note 9 is the key admission: **"Search services are not required under the Act to use highly effective age assurance to prevent access to pornographic content by children."** <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/age-checks-helping-make-online-experiences-safer-for-uk-children-but-job-not-done-and-tech-industry-must-act-to-strengthen-protections> (confirmed).
- **Ofcom, Use of Age Assurance Report 2026, 15 July 2026:** "Following engagement, Google and Bing have agreed to work with Ofcom and other relevant stakeholders to explore practical solutions to address discoverability of porn services without protections via search engines." No binding measure is announced. <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/use-of-age-assurance-report-2026> (confirmed).
- **McGlynn, Woods & Antoniou (2024):** "A considerable concern lies in the comparatively weaker obligations on search services, given their foundational role in content access, including pornography." They note that search services' duty is to minimise risk, not to prevent access, and that this is "a reduced obligation compared to user-to-user services". (confirmed academic analysis).
- **DSIT, enactment impact assessment, 23 October 2024, para 87:** "The pornography provision does not capture user-to-user content or search results presented on a search service, as the draft OSA regulates these separately (under the Act's core duties)." (confirmed official rationale).
- **Ofcom 2026 report, note 8:** "Other protections" includes geo-blocking UK users, so the 33%/54% figures may understate the number of results leading to *unprotected* content (some may be geo-blocked rather than age-assured). (confirmed methodological caveat).

**Status:** The statutory exclusion and the absence of a search-specific HEAA duty are **confirmed**. The policy criticism is **confirmed** by Ofcom's own data and its note 9. Whether Part 3 duties are adequate is **contested**; no enforcement proceedings against Google or Bing over search discoverability were found in the sources reviewed.

**Gaps/unknowns:** No search-specific HEAA duty; no enforcement against search engines; no published metric for what counts as "practical solutions"; whether search autocomplete/predictive results are covered by the same exclusion; whether the 33%/54% test replicated logged-out UK users only (note 7 says it did); whether children's actual search behaviour matches the test queries.

## 6. Text-only and paid-advertisement exclusions (s 79(4)–(5))

**Description:** Content consisting only of text (or text plus a non-pornographic GIF, emoji or symbol) is excluded from "regulated provider pornographic content", as are paid-for advertisements. The same text-only exclusion appears in the Part 3 definition of primary priority content (s 61(6)), so text-based pornography is outside age-assurance duties across the regime.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 79(4):** content is excluded if it "(a) consists only of text, or (b) consists only of text accompanied by— (i) a GIF which is not itself pornographic content, (ii) an emoji or other symbol, or (iii) a combination …". (confirmed).
- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 79(5):** content is excluded if it "consists of a paid-for advertisement (see section 236)". (confirmed).
- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 236(1):** "paid-for advertisement" requires (a) the provider receives consideration (monetary or non-monetary), directly or indirectly, and (b) placement is determined by systems or processes agreed between the parties to the contract. (confirmed).
- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 61(6):** the Part 3 "primary priority content" definition excludes pornographic content that "consists only of text" (with equivalent GIF/emoji/identifying-content carve-outs). So the text-only gap is not a Part 5-only anomaly. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/61> (confirmed).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, para 3.11:** lists both exclusions and says they are "expressly excluded". Para 3.10 says the definition otherwise "encompasses content in a range of forms, including still and moving images, audio and audio-visual content". (confirmed).
- **No published rationale found:** neither the Ofcom guidance nor the DSIT impact assessment explains why text-only pornography is excluded. The impact assessment discusses the UGC/search carve-outs but not the text carve-out. (gap in the record).

**Status:** The exclusions are **confirmed** in statute and regulator guidance. Their exploitation is **speculation/analytical**: no provider has publicly claimed to restructure content as text to escape the regime, and no Ofcom enforcement or guidance addresses the boundary. The paid-ad definition is narrow enough that affiliate/organic promotion may fall outside it (see §13.G).

**Gaps/unknowns:** No definition of "text" (does AI-generated chat count? subtitles? alt text?); no guidance on text-plus-image hybrids designed to fall outside (e.g. a single non-pornographic GIF); no guidance on audio erotica (which Ofcom says *is* covered) vs text erotica (which is not); no evidence on the size of the text/audio porn market; no consideration of AI companions that generate text.

## 7. "Pornographic content" definition (s 236) and borderline cases: art, education, AI companions, audio, text

**Description:** "Pornographic content" means content "of such a nature that it is reasonable to assume that it was produced solely or principally for the purpose of sexual arousal". The test is objective and purpose-based, but it is applied to a very wide range of material. Art, education, AI companions, audio erotica and text create difficult boundary cases.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 236(1):** "pornographic content" means "content of such a nature that it is reasonable to assume that it was produced solely or principally for the purpose of sexual arousal". <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/236> (confirmed).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, paras 3.5–3.10:** the definition may include "artificial images, such as AI generated or animated images"; it "might include content which would fall under the BBFC's R18 category", but "other content of a strong sexual nature that seeks to sexually arouse or stimulate, that would not fall in scope of this classification, may also be treated as pornographic"; the definition "encompasses … audio and audio-visual content". (confirmed).
- **McGlynn, Woods & Antoniou (2024):** the definition is the anchor for the Act's content-based approach; their central finding is that the Act "generates eight new classifications of pornography, each associated with a distinct legal framework, thereby creating a confusing and unnecessarily complex regulatory regime". They expressly say they do not examine Part 5, so the eight classifications are a Part 3/illegal-content critique, not a Part 5 map. (confirmed academic analysis).
- **Ofcom, Use of Age Assurance Report 2026:** notes the growth of AI-generated content and the need for whole-system protections; Ofcom's GenAI guidance treats tools "capable of generating pornography" as potentially in scope (see §11). (confirmed).
- **5Rights, "5Rights' Coalition helps secure EU ban on nudifying AI but gaps remain", 12 May 2026:** the EU has banned nudifying/sexualising AI systems and systems that create CSAM; 5Rights says the ban covers providers and deployers, including systems "without effective safeguards". This is an EU comparison, not a statement about UK law. <https://5rightsfoundation.com/5rights-coalition-help-secure-eu-ban-on-nudifying-ai-but-gaps-remain/> (confirmed as EU development; UK gap is analytical).
- **No case law found:** no UK judgment construing "solely or principally for the purpose of sexual arousal" in the OSA context. (gap).

**Status:** The definition is **confirmed**. Borderline application is **contested/uncertain**. The art/education point is largely **analytical**: the "reasonable to assume" formula may exclude serious artistic or educational material, but there is no guidance or case law on where the line falls. Audio is **confirmed** as within scope by Ofcom, which makes the text-only exclusion look arbitrary.

**Gaps/unknowns:** No judicial interpretation; no guidance on art, sex education, medical/clinical imagery, or documentary material; no guidance on AI companions that generate romantic/sexual text or audio; no guidance on "audio erotica" services (Ofcom says audio is covered, but the practical application is untested); no data on enforcement against audio/text services.

## 8. "Normally able to encounter" (s 81(2)) and "highly effective" (s 81(3)): undefined thresholds and the "method is enough" criticism

**Description:** Section 81 requires that children are "not normally able to encounter" regulated provider pornography, using age assurance that is "highly effective" at determining whether a user is a child. Neither phrase is defined by a metric. Ofcom accepts that some children may circumvent checks. Critics argue this lets services comply by adopting an approved method without demonstrating outcomes.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 81(2)–(3):** the duty is to ensure, by age verification or estimation, "that children are not normally able to encounter" the content; the method must be "of such a kind, and used in such a way, that it is highly effective at correctly determining whether or not a particular user is a child". <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/81> (confirmed).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, para 4.8:** "Implementing one of the example methods is not a guarantee that the service is acting in accordance with the requirements of the Act – service providers need to be able to [demonstrate use in practice]." (confirmed).
- **Same guidance, para 4.45:** "We acknowledge that it may be possible for some children to circumvent the age assurance process or access control mechanisms that the provider has put in place to meet its duties. However, risks can be mitigated by service providers taking steps to improve the robustness of their age assurance process." (confirmed).
- **Internet Matters, response to Ofcom's Part 5 consultation (2023/24):** "We are concerned that the current guidance leaves the interpretation of 'not normally able to encounter' with pornography providers. Given the principles-led approach that Ofcom has decided to adopt with this guidance, we are worried that this leaves a high degree of interpretation and undue level of leniency for regulated providers." It calls for "a strong definition". <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/consultations/category-1-10-weeks/272586-consultation-guidance-for-service-providers-publishing-pornographic-content/responses/internet-matters.pdf?v=369422> (alleged/advocacy, but directly on point).
- **Online Safety Act Network, response to Ofcom's Part 5 consultation:** identifies "the lack of specificity as to what 'highly effective' means and the risk that, in not specifying this at the outset of the regime, the industry will comply with what is a much lower bar than that intended by the legislation and will then settle at that baseline in years to come." It also notes that s 81(2) is "not subject to a specific proportionality requirement – and this is by contrast to the position in relation to the Part 3 safety duties." <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/consultations/category-1-10-weeks/272586-consultation-guidance-for-service-providers-publishing-pornographic-content/responses/online-safety-act-network.pdf?v=368287> (confirmed analysis).
- **5Rights / Children's Coalition, Enforcing the Online Safety Act for Children, 1 April 2024:** "'Tick-box' solutions are insufficient – instead, Ofcom must focus on measurable outcomes that demonstrably enhance children's online safety." <https://5rightsfoundation.com/resource/enforcing-online-safety-act-for-children/> (alleged/advocacy).
- **Julian Petley, "Porn and the OSA: what about the children?", Porn Studies 12(3) (2025) 362–386:** the article is described in the journal's editorial as "a forensic examination of the UK's Online Safety Act 2023"; a search-index snippet from the article criticises "simply labelling its approved methods 'highly effective'". DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1080/23268743.2025.2476961> (alleged/critical academic commentary; full text paywalled and not independently retrieved — treat the snippet with caution).
- **Handley Gill, 25 September 2026:** Ofcom's investigation into Aylo/Pornhub over its reliance on Apple's one-off device-level Age Confirmation checks "calls into question … the acceptability of relying on one-off device level age checks to provide highly effective age assurance, and whether the robustness criterion can be met", because "once a device has been verified, any user of that device can access apps intended for over 18s without further checks". <https://www.handleygill.co.uk/handley-gill-blog/online-safety-act-2023-highly-effective-age-assurance-ofcom-pornhub-investigation-announced> (confirmed as description of the Ofcom investigation; the outcome is contested/pending).
- **Ofcom, "Ofcom launches investigation into Pornhub's age checks", 23 September 2026:** Ofcom says it is concerned that Aylo "may not have conducted sufficient due diligence and testing before implementing" Apple's checks, and that the process "may not be highly effective". <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/ofcom-launches-investigation-into-pornhubs-age-checks> (confirmed).

**Status:** The absence of a metric is **confirmed**. The "compliant merely by having a method" criticism is **alleged/contested**: Ofcom's guidance expressly says a listed method is not a guarantee, and the Aylo investigation shows Ofcom will test implementation. But the criticism has force because the statutory test is outcome-based ("not normally able") while the guidance is method-and-criteria-based, and no enforcement decision has yet articulated a quantitative threshold.

**Gaps/unknowns:** No definition of "normally" (statistical? frequency? proportion of children?); no minimum accuracy/error-rate; no published enforcement decision setting a threshold; no litigation was found; whether device-level checks satisfy "robustness"; whether "some children may circumvent" is being used as a de facto safe harbour; no data on the gap between method adoption and measured child access.

## 9. On-demand programme services carve-out (s 80(6)) and the boundary with TV-like VOD

**Description:** Part 5 does not apply to a part of a service that is an on-demand programme service (ODPS), or a non-UK ODPS that is a Tier 1 service. ODPS are regulated under Part 4A of the Communications Act 2003, where the child-protection standard is "proportionate measures", not "highly effective" age assurance. A pornographic subscription service structured as an ODPS may therefore face a lower standard.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 80(6), (6A):** "This Part does not apply in relation to a part of a regulated service if that part is an on-demand programme service or a non-UK on-demand programme service that is a Tier 1 service." Definitions are in ss 368A, 368AA and 368HA of the Communications Act 2003. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/80> (confirmed).
- **Schedule 9, paras 5–6:** exempts an entire service that is an ODPS/Tier 1 non-UK ODPS, and a part of a service that is an ODPS where no regulated provider pornographic content is published on the rest of the service. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/schedule/9> (confirmed).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, paras 3.26–3.27:** "Some providers of pornographic content may provide such content through an 'on-demand programme service.' This might include, for instance, a pornographic subscription service where editorial decisions about that service are made in the UK and the provider of the service has its head office in the UK. Part 5 does not apply in relation to a service to the extent it is an on-demand programme service … On-demand programme services are regulated under Part 4A of the 2003 Act, and Ofcom has set out Rules and Guidance for providers of on-demand programme services." (confirmed).
- **DSIT, enactment impact assessment, 23 October 2024, para 115:** "VoDs are not in scope of this OSA as they are already subject to existing regulation (the UK's transposition of the Audiovisual Media Services Directive) which includes protecting children from pornography as well as wider duties…" (confirmed official rationale).
- **McGlynn, Woods & Antoniou (2024):** note that Part 4A of the Communications Act 2003 regulates TV-like on-demand programme services and that Ofcom has used suspension powers against a "TV-like" video-on-demand service offering R18-equivalent content without a robust paywall (the "Jessica Presley" case). They also note the regime was under review via the Media Bill. (confirmed academic/regulatory context).
- **Media Act 2024 amendments:** s 80(6) was amended by the Media Act 2024 to add "non-UK on-demand programme service that is a Tier 1 service", extending the carve-out to certain non-UK TV-like services. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/15/schedule/7/paragraph/5> (confirmed).

**Status:** The carve-out is **confirmed**. The risk of regulatory arbitrage — a porn service restructuring its editorial decision-making/head office to qualify as an ODPS and avoid HEAA — is **speculation/analytical**. No published case of a porn site successfully claiming ODPS status to escape Part 5 was found.

**Gaps/unknowns:** The ODPS definition is fact-specific (editorial responsibility, head office, TV-like nature); no Ofcom decision on a porn service's ODPS status was found; no evidence on how many adult services are ODPS; whether the "proportionate measures" standard is materially weaker in practice; interaction with the Tier 1 designation; whether a service can split itself into an ODPS part and a non-ODPS part to shield content.

## 10. "Acting on behalf of the provider" (s 79(2)) and aggregators describing themselves as "search engines" (s 229)

**Description:** Part 5 applies to content published by the provider "or by a person acting on behalf of the provider". "Acting on behalf of" is undefined. Separately, some adult aggregators describe themselves as "search engines"; if accepted, that would shift them toward Part 3 search duties and away from Part 5. Section 229 defines "search engine" broadly but excludes a service that searches just one website or database.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 79(2):** "provider pornographic content" includes content published or displayed "by the provider of the service or by a person acting on behalf of the provider", including via software, automated tools or algorithms applied by either. No definition of "acting on behalf". (confirmed).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, paras 3.14–3.16:** the provider is "the entity or individual that has control over which content is published or displayed"; Ofcom is likely to find control where there is "editorial control over the nature, selection or presentation of the content". This is a functional test, not an agency test. (confirmed).
- **BBFC/Ofcom research, p 5:** "Some aggregator services describe themselves as search engines. This may have implications in terms of which parts of the Bill relate to this type of service." Aggregators were 18% of the top 100. (confirmed observation).
- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 229:** "'search engine'— (a) includes a service or functionality which enables a person to search some websites or databases (as well as … all websites or databases); (b) does not include a service which enables a person to search just one website or database." s 229(2): a search engine is not "included" in a service if it is controlled by a person who does not control other parts of the service. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/229> (confirmed).
- **Combined services:** s 4(7) defines a "combined service" as a regulated U2U service that includes a public search engine; Schedule 2 addresses U2U/search services that include regulated provider pornographic content. A service can be regulated under more than one route. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/schedule/2> (confirmed).
- **OSA Network response to Ofcom's Part 5 consultation:** quotes the "person acting on behalf of the provider" wording and treats it as part of the scope definition without further analysis — an indication that the concept is under-examined in the commentary. (confirmed as a gap in analysis).

**Status:** The agency ambiguity is **confirmed** textually. The aggregator/"search engine" characterisation is **confirmed as an observed market practice** by the BBFC, but its legal effect is **contested/unresolved**: no Ofcom decision or court ruling on whether a particular adult aggregator is a search service. The s 229(1)(b) single-site exclusion limits the label, but many aggregators search multiple sites.

**Gaps/unknowns:** No definition of "acting on behalf"; no case on affiliate/white-label/embedded arrangements was found; no Ofcom decision on aggregator classification was found; whether an aggregator that labels itself a search engine still has Part 5 duties for provider content it displays; whether the "search results" exclusion in s 79(6)(b) then shields it; enforcement risk if the label is rejected.

## 11. AI-generated porn, "nudify" apps, and model providers that do not publish output

**Description:** Section 79(6)(a)(iii) treats pornographic content generated on a service by an automated tool in response to a user prompt as "published or displayed", even if only visible to that user for a moment. Ofcom says the person who makes the tool available is the provider. But model/API providers that supply weights or inference without publishing output, and AI companions that generate text or audio, sit awkwardly within the definition.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 79(6)(a)(iii):** "published or displayed" includes "pornographic content that is generated on the service by means of an automated tool or algorithm in response to a prompt by a user and is only visible or audible to that user (no matter for how short a time)". (confirmed).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, paras 3.19–3.24:** "if an online service provider makes available a GenAI tool that is designed to be used for the purpose of generating pornography, this aspect of the service would fall in scope of Part 5"; if a general tool is "capable of generating pornography", the provider must determine that and can implement safeguards (keyword blockers, classifiers, removing porn from training data, red-teaming); "solely including a provision in the terms of service … would not secure the outcome of preventing the type of content being produced, and would therefore not be deemed a sufficient safeguard"; if users can share generated content with other users, that aspect is "more likely to fall within … Part 3". (confirmed).
- **Ofcom, "Ofcom fines nudification site £50,000 for failing to introduce age-checks", 20 November 2025:** Itai Tech Ltd, operator of Undress.cc, fined £50,000 for breaching s 81, plus £5,000 for failing to comply with an information request; the fine took account of the site making itself unavailable to UK IP addresses shortly after the investigation opened. <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/ofcom-fines-nudification-site-50000-for-failing-to-introduce-age-checks> (confirmed).
- **BBC, "Deepfake 'nudify' site fined £55,000 over lack of age checks", 20 November 2025:** confirms the enforcement and that it was Ofcom's second fine under the regime. <https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c0qpq5q9712o> (confirmed).
- **5Rights, 12 May 2026 (EU):** the EU has banned nudifying/sexualising AI systems and systems that create CSAM, covering providers and deployers and systems "without effective safeguards". This is a comparative benchmark: the UK's OSA route depends on the tool being made available "on" a service and on the content being "published or displayed". (confirmed as EU law; UK comparison analytical).
- **Ofcom, Use of Age Assurance Report 2026:** Ofcom says "there is no single age assurance method or process that eliminates the risk of circumvention" and calls for layered protections including app stores, operating systems and device-level measures. (confirmed).

**Status:** Nudify *sites* are **confirmed** to be in scope (Itai Tech enforcement). Whether base-model/API providers that do not publish output are in scope is **speculation/unresolved**: Ofcom's guidance focuses on the service that "makes available" the tool, and s 79(6)(a)(iii) requires generation "on the service". AI companions that generate text are likely outside Part 5 because of the text-only exclusion; audio companions may be inside. The claim that 5Rights specifically flagged the UK OSA gap was **not verified**; 5Rights' documented work is on the EU AI Act/Digital Omnibus.

**Gaps/unknowns:** No enforcement against a model/API provider; no guidance on open-weight models; no guidance on AI companions; whether generated content that is not retained is "published or displayed"; whether a tool that is "capable" of porn but not designed for it is in scope in practice; how Ofcom would enforce against an offshore API provider; interaction with the text-only exclusion.

## 12. Schedule 1 / Schedule 9 exemptions and internal business services

**Description:** Part 5 does not apply to services exempt under Schedule 1 or Schedule 9, including internal business services, public bodies, education/childcare services and ODPS. Schedule 1 paragraph 6 removes most Schedule 1 exemptions where regulated provider pornographic content is present, but the internal-business and closed-group concepts remain potentially broad.

**What we found**

- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 80(3), (5):** a service is "exempt" if it is a U2U/search service exempt under Schedule 1 or an internet service of a kind described in Schedule 9; Part 5 does not apply to a part of a service meeting the internal business service conditions in Schedule 1 para 7(2) (Part 3 service) or Schedule 9 para 1(2) (other service). (confirmed).
- **Schedule 9, paras 1–4:** exempts (1) an entire internet service that is an "internal resource or tool for a business" available only to a closed group of officers/workers/authorised persons (including contractors, consultants, auditors, and in educational institutions pupils/students); (2) a part of a service meeting those conditions where no regulated provider porn is on the rest; (3) services provided by public bodies in the exercise of public functions; (4) services provided by education/childcare providers for those purposes. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/schedule/9> (confirmed).
- **Bratby Law, 29 August 2026:** "Paragraph 6 removes all of these exemptions where regulated provider pornographic content is published or displayed on the service and the service has links with the UK within section 80(4): a Part 5 service cannot rely on the email or limited-functionality exemptions." It also explains the closed-group internal business exemption and the s 55 carve-outs. <https://bratby.law/does-the-online-safety-act-apply/> (confirmed).
- **Primary text — OSA 2023, s 55(2):** "regulated user-generated content" excludes emails, SMS, MMS, one-to-one live aural communications, comments and reviews on provider content, identifying content, and news publisher content. This is a Part 3 carve-out, but it matters to the overall porn regime: live one-to-one audio sexual services are not regulated UGC. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/55> (confirmed).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, para 3.28:** the Schedule 1/Schedule 9 exemptions principally remove "internal business services (e.g., intranet services) from Part 5, provided they meet the specified requirements". (confirmed).

**Status:** The exemptions are **confirmed**. Their abuse is **speculation**: no evidence was found of a commercial porn service claiming to be an "internal business service" or an education service. The closed-group condition and the "no regulated provider porn on the rest of the service" condition limit the obvious routes.

**Gaps/unknowns:** No guidance on how "internal resource or tool for a business" applies to multi-tenant enterprise platforms or closed adult communities; no guidance on the education exemption for university-run archives/teaching materials; no evidence on use of the one-to-one live aural communications carve-out by commercial audio sex services; no data on exempt services that nonetheless carry porn.

## 13. New loopholes and structural gaps not on the list

### A. VPN and geo-circumvention (confirmed, widely reported)

- **The Verge, 25 July 2025:** age checks rely heavily on IP geolocation; a VPN "allows users to bypass age checks entirely"; ad blockers and third-party clients are additional workarounds. <https://www.theverge.com/analysis/713773/uk-online-safety-act-age-verification-bypass-vpn> (confirmed reporting).
- **The Independent, 19 August 2025:** the Children's Commissioner urged action on VPNs; her report found 70% of respondents had seen pornography before 18 (up from 64% two years earlier) and 59% encountered it accidentally. <https://www.independent.co.uk/bulletin/news/online-safety-checks-vpns-government-b2810190.html> (confirmed reporting).
- **Ofcom, Use of Age Assurance Report 2026 / Canary, 5 May 2026:** circumvention is broader than VPNs: fake birthdate (13%), someone else's login (9%), another person's device (8%), VPN only 7% in the cited survey. <https://www.thecanary.co/trending/2026/05/05/age-verification-bypass/> (confirmed survey reporting; note the survey is not Ofcom's own).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, para 4.45:** accepts some children may circumvent; expects providers to mitigate "easily accessible" methods. (confirmed).

### B. Device-level and shared-device checks (contested)

- **Handley Gill, 25 September 2026:** one-off Apple Age Confirmation checks mean "a child who obtains access to their parent's or another adult's device would be able to access adult content using that device"; Ofcom's Aylo investigation tests the "robustness" criterion. (confirmed as description; outcome pending).
- **Aylo response (quoted by Handley Gill):** Apple's device-level update "renders devices operating since iOS26.4 kid safe by default" and offers "one of the strongest and hardest to circumvent protections currently available". (contested).

### C. Age inference on social media (confirmed regulator concern)

- **Ofcom, 15 July 2026:** "serious doubts whether age inference methods currently used by some popular social media sites and apps are fit for purpose"; TikTok investigation launched; age inference is "not … included in our guidance as methods that are capable of being highly effective". (confirmed).
- Relevance to porn: children may encounter pornographic content on general social media before/without age assurance; the Part 5 regime does not reach those services' feeds.

### D. Search discoverability and predictive search (confirmed)

- Covered in §5. Additional point: Ofcom's 2026 report says children move quickly between sites, "potentially in search of a site they could access without an age check" (note 4). Search is the discovery layer that the Act does not age-gate.

### E. Offshore hosting, geo-blocking and enforcement reach (confirmed/contested)

- **Ofcom, 15 July 2026:** 64 of the top 100 porn services had age assurance by June 2026; a further 10 geo-blocked UK users; 23 investigations into 88 services; 7 providers/24 sites fined (8579 LLC £1.35m; AVS Group £1m; Kick Online £800k; Fapello provider £630k; Youngtek £500k; First Time Videos £80k; Itai Tech £50k). <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/age-checks-helping-make-online-experiences-safer-for-uk-children-but-job-not-done-and-tech-industry-must-act-to-strengthen-protections> (confirmed).
- **Linklaters, "UK – The Online Safety Act 2023 – the landscape two years on", September 2025:** notes the 4chan jurisdictional tussle and that "many of the services that pose the greatest risk … are likely to have no physical presence in the UK". <https://www.linklaters.com/en/insights/blogs/digilinks/2025/september/uk-the-online-safety-act-2023-the-landscape-two-years-on> (confirmed analysis).
- **Ofcom, "Ofcom fines nudification site…", 20 November 2025:** the fine was reduced to reflect the site geo-blocking the UK — i.e. exit, rather than compliance, is treated as mitigating. (confirmed).
- **Implication (analytical):** geo-blocking removes the service from UK users but does not protect children who use VPNs or travel; it also creates a "compliance by exit" dynamic.

### F. Payment processors and app stores as indirect enforcement (confirmed/analytical)

- **OSA 2023, ss 144–146:** courts may make service restriction orders against ancillary services (payment providers, advertisers) and access restriction orders against internet access services and app stores. (confirmed).
- **ppc.land, c. July 2025:** describes Visa/Mastercard/PayPal as "the primary enforcement mechanism" and the Act as turning processors into "active government enforcement agents"; notes the 1,400% VPN surge. <https://ppc.land/visa-and-mastercard-become-enforcement-arms-of-uk-online-safety-regulations/> (confirmed reporting/analysis; some claims are strong).
- **Ofcom, 15 July 2026, note 13:** Ofcom will publish a statutory report on app-store protections by January 2027, assessing "what role app stores play … whether children's online safety would be enhanced by greater use of age assurance … by app store providers". (confirmed).
- **Analytical:** payment/app-store pressure can bypass the statutory scope question entirely, but it is discretionary, opaque and not a substitute for a duty on the service.

### G. Affiliate networks and organic promotion (speculation/analytical)

- The s 236 "paid-for advertisement" definition requires consideration **and** placement determined by agreed systems/processes. Affiliate links, creator promo codes, "shop window" channel pages and organic posts may not meet both limbs. The BBFC report describes tube channels as "a 'shop window' through which the production company/creator will attempt to direct users to their own external paid-for provider content" (p 17). No source was found applying the paid-ad definition to affiliate arrangements. (speculation/analytical).

### H. "Eight classifications" and regulatory complexity (confirmed academic critique)

- **McGlynn, Woods & Antoniou (2024):** the Act "generates eight new classifications of pornography, each associated with a distinct legal framework, thereby creating a confusing and unnecessarily complex regulatory regime"; "the boundaries between classifications are uncertain, resulting in a complex and potentially confusing regulatory regime". They expressly exclude Part 5 from their analysis, so this is a critique of the Part 3/illegal-content architecture, but it reinforces the arbitrage risk across the whole regime. (confirmed).
- **Their related point on ToS minimalism:** "there is no minimum content requirement for these ToS in the Act. Accordingly, many services could simply re-write their terms in the most minimal way, thus enabling them to claim they comply … without removing any material currently available." (confirmed academic analysis; applies to Part 3 Category 1 duties).

### I. Combined and hybrid services (confirmed statutory structure; arbitrage speculative)

- **OSA 2023, s 4(7) and Schedule 2:** a U2U service with a public search engine is a "combined service"; Schedule 2 brings certain U2U/search services that include regulated provider pornographic content into the regime. A service can be regulated under multiple routes. (confirmed).
- **Ofcom, Overview of regulated services:** "If a service provides more than one of these categories of service (for instance, it is a user to user service and a search service), it will be subject to all of the associated legal requirements for those categories." <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/online-safety/information-for-industry/illegal-harms/overview-of-regulated-services.pdf?v=387540> (confirmed).
- **Analytical:** the more routes, the more room for a provider to argue that a particular slice of functionality is on the least burdensome route (e.g. "search results" under s 79(6)(b), UGC under s 79(7), ODPS under s 80(6)).

### J. One-to-one live aural communications (confirmed carve-out; exploitation speculative)

- **OSA 2023, s 55(2)(d), (5):** "regulated user-generated content" excludes "one-to-one live aural communications" — real-time speech or sounds between two users, not a recording, not accompanied by other UGC. A live audio sexual service between two users would not be regulated UGC; Part 5 only reaches provider audio. (confirmed text; application untested).
- **Ofcom Part 5 guidance, para 3.10:** provider audio *is* within Part 5. The asymmetry between provider audio and user-to-user live audio is a potential gap.

### K. Text-only across the regime (confirmed carve-out; exploitation speculative)

- Covered in §6. The same exclusion in s 61(6) means text-based pornography is outside both Part 5 and the Part 3 primary-priority-content duty. AI-generated erotic text/chat is the most obvious growth area. (confirmed text; market impact unknown).

### L. Territorial limitation in s 80(8) (confirmed text; effect contested)

- **OSA 2023, s 80(8):** "The duties set out in section 81 extend only to the design, operation and use of an internet service in the United Kingdom." This limits the duty's territorial reach and may complicate enforcement against wholly offshore design/operation decisions. (confirmed).
- **Ofcom, Overview of regulated services:** "The duties in the Act apply to providers of services with links to the UK regardless of where in the world they are based." (confirmed regulator position). The tension between s 80(8) and extraterritorial enforcement is **contested/analytical**.

### M. Small high-harm platforms and categorisation (confirmed policy gap, Part 3)

- **Antoniou, 19 October 2023:** size-based categorisation left "a large gap in a risk-based regime"; the Lords amended the Bill to make Category 1 a size **or** functionality test. This is about Part 3 categorisation, not Part 5 (which has no size threshold), but it shows the regime's recurring struggle to catch small high-harm services. (confirmed).
- **Ofcom, 2026 report:** enforcement is prioritising services "growing their user numbers as a result of failure to put age checks in place" — an explicit recognition of migration to non-compliant services. (confirmed).

### N. "Compliance by exit" and market migration (confirmed)

- **Ofcom, 15 July 2026:** "Many porn sites with age checks in place have experienced sharp declines in traffic, while some without have gained in popularity"; 4% of children visited at least one porn service with no age checks; the top 100 list is dynamic. (confirmed).
- **Analytical:** the regime may displace demand to non-compliant/offshore services rather than reduce children's access; Ofcom's own data on short visit durations (87% under 30 seconds) is consistent with site-hopping in search of an unprotected site.

## 15. Headline assessment

The strongest, best-evidenced vulnerabilities are:

1. **Search (§5)** — a confirmed statutory exclusion, admitted by Ofcom in note 9, with hard 2026 data (33% Google / 54% Bing / 62% of children using search first). This is the clearest gap between the Act's child-protection objective and its legal reach.
2. **UGC/tube-site boundary (§3–4)** — confirmed by statute and by Ofcom's own research showing it could not always distinguish provider from UGC; the January/July 2025 timing gap is real, and the Pornhub allegation is well-reported though not directly proven.
3. **Undefined thresholds (§1, §8)** — confirmed textual indeterminacy; the "significant number"/"target market" and "normally able"/"highly effective" tests have no metric, and enforcement is still nascent.
4. **Text-only and paid-ad exclusions (§6)** — confirmed and under-discussed; the text-only carve-out is duplicated in Part 3, so it is a regime-wide hole rather than a Part 5 quirk.
5. **AI/nudify (§11)** — nudify sites are confirmed in scope and have been fined, but model/API providers and AI companions are untested; the EU has moved to an explicit ban, which highlights the UK's reliance on the "published or displayed" formula.

The weakest or most speculative points are: the s 80(4) "small audience" escape (§1) — structurally real but no documented use; the ODPS carve-out (§9) — real but no known porn-sector arbitrage; the internal-business exemptions (§12) — real but no evidence of abuse; and affiliate networks (§13.G) — analytically plausible but no source applying the paid-ad definition.

**Overall:** the Part 5 regime is not so much a single loophole as a set of **scope boundaries that were drawn for administrative convenience and now determine whether a child is protected**. The most consequential are the search exclusion, the provider/UGC line, and the absence of any metric for "highly effective". Each is confirmed in the statute or by Ofcom; the extent to which providers have deliberately exploited them is, in most cases, still alleged rather than proven.
