# Enforcement and extraterritoriality weaknesses in the UK Online Safety Act age-verification regime

**Research dossier — evidence only. Not the final report.**
**Prepared:** 2 November 2026
**Scope:** Part 5 (ss 79–82) age-verification duty (in force 17 January 2025); Part 3 children's duties (in force 25 July 2025); Ofcom enforcement, penalties, business disruption measures, and cross-border collection.

> **Note on the 4chan litigation.** A separate subagent is covering *4chan v Ofcom* in depth. This dossier uses 4chan only as an illustration of the collection and jurisdiction problem, and does not attempt a full case analysis.

## 1. Fines are effectively uncollectable: two providers paid, £135,000 of over £7m

**Description.** The penalty regime's deterrent effect depends on money actually changing hands. Eighteen months in, Ofcom has collected about 1.9% of the nominal value of its OSA fines, almost all from two small providers. Ofcom's own witnesses concede that the majority will never be paid and that foreign courts may block recovery altogether.

### What we found

- **Ofcom's formal written admission (primary source).** Letter from Oliver Griffiths, Group Director, Online Safety, to Baroness Keeley, 6 October 2026:
  > "Fines are one of the key methods in our compliance toolkit and to date we have levied **31 fines against 11 providers, totalling over £7 million**. … Currently, **only two providers have paid their fines, totalling £135,000**."
  — <https://committees.parliament.uk/publications/55484/standard/pdf/>

- **Dame Melanie Dawes to the Commons DCMS Committee, 20 October 2026.** She broke the 11 down:
  > "We have issued 11 fines. Two have been paid, one is being appealed, and one is not yet overdue. Seven fines are overdue. Of the nine that are unpaid in total, two are porn companies that have now implemented highly effective age assurance, and another four have geo-blocked the UK. That leaves **three that are still available without the risks being mitigated**, out of the 11 services that we fined."
  — HC 528, Q59, <https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/18155/html/>

- **The concession that some fines will never be recovered.**
  > "We will not be able to recover every single fine on online safety. If foreign courts decide to try to get in our way, there will be a point at which we have to accept that we will not be able to recover that money, but we will always seek to go as far as we can."
  — Dawes, ibid.

- **LBC's story (20 October 2026).** "Ofcom boss admits tech giants have dodged nine out of 11 online safety fines." The article records the same two-paid/one-appealed/one-not-due/seven-overdue breakdown and quotes Dawes on the three still-available services.
  — <https://www.lbc.co.uk/article/ofcom-tech-firms-fine-mps-5Hjdk9J_2>

- **Earlier LBC exposé (9 March 2026).** "Adult sites brazenly 'ignore' Ofcom fines: Watchdog receives just £55k after imposing £3m fines." At that point only Itai Tech had paid. Suzanne Cater told LBC:
  > "It is obviously more challenging when services are based overseas, and we do see that a lot in the online sphere, more so than in the telecom space."
  — <https://www.lbc.co.uk/article/ofcom-fines-55-million-child-age-5HjdW55_2>

- **Ofcom's enforcement director to the Lords, 15 September 2026.** Asked how much of £7m had been collected, Cater said:
  > "I do not have a figure to hand. … Realistically the majority has not been paid."
  — <https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/18091/html/>

- **Ofcom's public explainer (20 July 2026)** confirms the mechanics: at least 28 days to pay; if UK assets exist, Ofcom obtains a judgment debt and can use bailiffs; if not, it must "engage with law enforcement agencies and private specialists in other countries to identify individuals and assets".
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/what-happens-when-a-company-doesnt-pay-a-fine>

- **Reporting confirms the story internationally.** The Register, 17 September 2026: "Ofcom discovers issuing Online Safety Act fines is easier than collecting them"; Dexerto: "Ofcom admits it may never collect 4chan's fines"; Yahoo: "Ofcom's War on Illegal Content Is Hitting a Global Wall".
  — <https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/09/17/ofcom-discovers-issuing-online-safety-act-fines-is-easier-than-collecting-them/5297110>
  — <https://www.dexerto.com/entertainment/ofcom-admits-it-may-never-collect-4chans-fines-after-ai-hamster-trolling-3420509>

### Status
**Established and admitted by the regulator.** Ofcom says it has "initiated work regarding the pursuit of debt from all companies that have not paid their fines by their deadlines." No public report of a recovered OSA judgment debt has been found.

### Gaps / unknowns
- Ofcom has refused FOI requests to identify which providers have paid, citing confidentiality. The £135,000 figure implies Itai Tech (£55,000) and First Time Videos (£80,000), but Ofcom has not named them in the letter.
- No public register of judgment debts, writs, or overseas enforcement proceedings.
- The total value including daily-rate penalties is unknown; £7m appears to be fixed penalties only.
- Whether Ofcom has retained US counsel or started recognition proceedings is not disclosed.

### What we found

- **Ofcom's own running totals.** On 19 March 2026, alongside the 4chan decision, Ofcom said: "We have issued 16 fines under the Online Safety Act against six companies, totalling nearly £4 million, several of which have not yet passed their deadlines to pay."
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/4chan-fined-450000-for-not-protecting-children-from-online-pornography>

- **The 4chan package is three penalties, not one.** £450,000 for no age checks, £50,000 for no illegal-content risk assessment, £20,000 for terms of service. This is the only case where a provider has been fined for all three simultaneously, and it is the clearest example of "stacked" penalties that remain unpaid.
  — Ibid.

- **Fapello's £630,000 is £600,000 + £30,000.** The £600,000 is the s 12 age-assurance penalty; the £30,000 is for failing to respond to a formal information request. Ofcom's press release and BBC coverage both use £630,000.
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/ofcom-fines-porn-company-630000-for-age-check-failings>
  — <https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c07ylddnvmyo>

- **Youngtek's £600,000 is £500,000 + £100,000.** The £100,000 is unusually large for an information-request breach and reflects repeated non-response.
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/ofcom-fines-porn-company-600k>

- **Xgroovy is the most recent porn fine (September 2026).** £700,000 + £30,000. Ofcom's bulletin says the provider "has since introduced age checks and blocked access to the service from the UK."
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/online-safety-industry-bulletin-october-2026>

- **Only two payments are confirmed.** Ofcom's case updates state: "Itai Tech Ltd has paid the penalty set out in the Confirmation Decision. This investigation is now closed." First Time Videos' page states the same. No equivalent statement exists for any other provider.
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/enforcement-programme-to-protect-children-from-encountering-pornographic-content-through-the-use-of-age-assurance>
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/investigation-into-whether-first-time-videos-llc-has-failed-to-comply-with-the-online-safety-act-2023>

- **The 11-provider count is coherent** only if im.ge and the suicide forum are included alongside the nine porn providers (Itai, AVS, Kick, 8579, 4chan, Youngtek, FTV, Fapello, Xgroovy). That gives 11. It also explains why the "porn" fines alone appear to be nine providers but Ofcom says 11. Corroboration: Ofcom's 19 March 2026 statement said it had issued "16 fines … against six companies"; at that date the six were Itai, AVS, Kick, 8579, 4chan and im.ge — which places im.ge inside the count.
  — Cross-checked against LBC, 9 March 2026 (im.ge £20k) and Ofcom suicide forum decision.

### Status
**Established.** Nine of 11 providers have not paid. One (Youngtek) is under appeal. Only two small providers have paid, totalling £135,000.

### Gaps / unknowns
- Which provider is "not yet overdue" is unclear. Xgroovy's 3 September decision would ordinarily be overdue by 20 October; the mismatch suggests either an extended deadline, a later penalty not in the public list, or a daily-rate notice.
- Whether 4chan's first £20,000 is counted within the "£520,000" or separately. Reporting based on Ofcom's June 2026 bulletin refers to a running total of about £540,000, which implies separate counting.
- Daily-rate penalties (e.g. £1,000/day for 8579, £500/day for 4chan) are not quantified in public totals.
- No public breakdown of legal costs incurred chasing each fine.

## 3. Geo-blocking is treated as compliance and counted as success — with VPNs left untouched

**Description.** Ofcom accepts a UK geo-block as a satisfactory outcome, sometimes closing investigations without any finding of breach. It counts geo-blocked sites within its "compliance" statistics, and its senior officials describe geo-blocking as a "win". The result is that a service can exit the UK market rather than implement age assurance, and still be counted as a success — while UK users with a VPN can continue to access it.

### What we found

- **Dawes expressly treats geo-blocking as a win.** Jeff Smith MP put it to her: "Dame Melanie, you said that you see the companies resorting to geo-blocking as a win." She did not dispute the characterisation, and answered by explaining the limits of the Act rather than the adequacy of geo-blocking.
  — HC 528, Q58, <https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/18155/html/>

- **Dawes describes fines as driving geo-blocking.** "When we levy fines, what is interesting is that that often drives the compliance measures being put in place, or a site geo-blocking the UK and removing it from being accessible to the British public."
  — HC 528, Q48.

- **Ofcom counts geo-blocks inside its top-100 compliance figure.** July 2026 report: "64 out of the top 100 most popular pornography services in the UK have deployed age assurance as of June 2026, with a further 10 geo-blocking UK users."
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/age-checks-helping-make-online-experiences-safer-for-uk-children-but-job-not-done-and-tech-industry-must-act-to-strengthen-protections>

- **The August 2026 figure is 63 + 11.** Ofcom's written evidence to the Lords: "Within the top 100 most used services, 63 have age assurance, and a further 11 have geo-blocked the UK – meaning 74% of these services are inaccessible to children in the UK without use of circumvention measures."
  — Ofcom written evidence IOS0039, <https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/169185/html/>

- **The corollary: 26 of the top 100 still had neither age assurance nor a geo-block** in both June and August 2026. Ofcom's "majority" framing conceals a quarter of the market.

- **Geo-blocking closes investigations without findings.** Duplanto Ltd (pornhaven.ai) was investigated, then closed on 30 March 2026 after it "restricted access to its service for people with UK IP addresses". Ofcom closed "without making any findings as to Duplanto Ltd's compliance with its duties." Trendio Ltd was similarly closed after providing information about "steps in good faith".
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/enforcement-programme-to-protect-children-from-encountering-pornographic-content-through-the-use-of-age-assurance>

- **Letters alone produce geo-blocks.** Ofcom's written evidence: it assessed over 1,000 porn websites, sent more than 500 letters, and "nearly a quarter of these letters result[ed] in immediate implementation of age assurance or a geo-block."
  — IOS0039.

- **The suicide forum is the paradigm.** Ofcom concluded that the forum's geoblock was "the fullest extent of what can currently be achieved by Ofcom under the Act", that "the legal grounds are therefore not met" for a business disruption order, and that "all possible legal routes under the Act have now been exhausted."
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/online-suicide-forum-investigation-and-review-of-enforcement-powers>

- **VPN circumvention is acknowledged and untreated.** Dawes:
  > "Our reading of the Act is that we do not have any capacity to require a website, for example, to block themselves in the United States just to deal with the risk of circumvention using a virtual private network by a UK citizen. We do not believe that the Act gives us that extraterritorial jurisdiction. … That means then that there is a risk that people will find a way around it and seek out harmful content, children will find pornography or whatever."
  — HC 528, Q58.

- **VPN use among children is substantial.** DSIT/BMG research published 14 July 2026: 26% of 11–17s have used a VPN in their lifetime; 22% in the last three months; 39% have successfully got around an age check at least once; 10% of all children say they have bypassed checks using settings or tools that change their apparent location; 53% have deliberately chosen a platform to avoid a check.
  — <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/childrens-circumvention-behaviours-online/childrens-circumvention-behaviours-online>

- **VPN demand spiked when the regime began.** Ofcom reported daily active VPN users temporarily doubled to ~1.5 million after July 2025, falling back to ~900,000 by November. One provider reported a 1,400% rise in UK sign-ups on 25 July 2025.
  — DSIT report, footnote 2; <https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/28/uk_vpn_demand_soars/>

- **Government has ruled out a VPN ban.** Reporting on Ofcom's position notes that the UK is "no longer considering a ban on VPNs". Dawes told MPs: "that is not something that western democracies have yet decided to do."
  — <https://www.biometricupdate.com/202607/social-media-companies-have-failed-to-enforce-their-minimum-age-requirements-ofcom>; HC 528, Q58.

### Status
**Structural and admitted.** Geo-blocking is an accepted compliance route, is counted in success metrics, and is not addressed by any VPN measure. Ofcom says the Act does not give it extraterritorial power to require global blocking.

### Gaps / unknowns
- No published count of how many services have geo-blocked versus implemented age assurance across the whole market (only the top 100).
- No published measurement of UK traffic to geo-blocked sites via VPN, or of whether geo-blocked services later return under a new domain.
- Whether Ofcom treats a geo-block as "compliance" for the purpose of closing a case, or merely as a reason not to prioritise enforcement, is not stated consistently.
- The 64+10 / 63+11 figures are not reconciled in public; the shift may reflect reclassification or site churn.

## 4. Capacity and speed: 23 investigations into 88 services, a quarter of the top 100 still unprotected, and "underwhelmed"

**Description.** Ofcom has been active relative to peer regulators, but the throughput is small against a market of at least 20,000 adult sites. Its own officials say the overall effect is "underwhelming"; the Children's Commissioner says children report "absolutely no difference". Full implementation of the Act is now not expected until mid-2027.

### What we found

- **The enforcement programme's own statistics.** "So far, we have opened 23 investigations into the providers of 88 adult services, resulting in the majority of services (73%) either implementing age assurance or blocking UK users. We have fined 7 providers of 24 sites so far."
  — Ofcom age assurance report, 15 July 2026, <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/age-checks-helping-make-online-experiences-safer-for-uk-children-but-job-not-done-and-tech-industry-must-act-to-strengthen-protections>

- **The long tail is enormous.** Ofcom told the Lords: "this is a dynamic market which includes a longtail of at least 20,000 identified adult sites and the 'top 100' changes often."
  — IOS0039.

- **Only about half of the 23 investigations had concluded by September 2026.** "Of the 23 investigations covering 88 porn sites, roughly half have concluded with the sites either blocking UK users or introducing age assurance."
  — IOS0039.

- **Ofcom's own verdict is "underwhelmed".** Oliver Griffiths, Group Director, Online Safety, to the Lords Communications and Digital Committee, 15 September 2026:
  > "Generally, when you look at our tracking indices on this, we are **underwhelmed by the overall effect so far**."
  — <https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/18091/html/>

- **City AM's headline.** "'Underwhelmed': Ofcom admits online safety impact falling short as full roll-out slips to 2027." The article records that the final categorised-services duties will not be fully in force until around mid-2027.
  — <https://www.cityam.com/underwhelmed-ofcom-admits-online-safety-impact-falling-short-as-full-roll-out-slips-to-2027>

- **The Children's Commissioner's verdict.** Dame Rachel de Souza told the same inquiry that children say the Act "has made absolutely no difference"; she called Ofcom "not effective enough", said she was "pretty furious" about being refused risk assessments, and urged Ofcom to "use its teeth" and impose "some big fines".
  — <https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/09/03/uks-online-safety-act-has-made-absolutely-no-difference-kids-say/5293893>
  — Oral evidence, 1 Sept 2026: <https://committees.parliament.uk/event/28058/formal-meeting-oral-evidence-session>

- **Activity metrics look strong but are inputs, not outcomes.** Over 69 million age checks were completed across a sample of 32 services between July and December 2025 — a 23-fold increase. The proportion of children asked to prove their age rose from 25% to 43%. But around seven in ten children still recalled encountering harmful content in a four-week period.
  — Ofcom report, 15 July 2026; IOS0039.

- **Staffing and workload.** Around 450 people work specifically on online safety. Ofcom has opened 40 formal investigations covering more than 100 services. Its chair says it will need more resources for the under-16 ban.
  — IOS0039; HC 528, Q1.

- **Ofcom says it is "fully loaded".** Griffiths: "We were fully loaded in terms of the amount of work we had to do before the social media ban was announced. … Without extra staff and funding, something will have to give."
  — City AM, 15 Sept 2026.

- **Speed of individual cases.** The suicide forum investigation ran from 9 April 2025 to 20 July 2026 — over 15 months — and ended without a court order. The Fapello investigation ran from November 2025 to July 2026. The Grok/X investigation opened 12 January 2026 and remains open at the time of writing.
  — Ofcom case pages; HC 528, Q56.

### Status
**Established.** Ofcom is more active than peer regulators but is not keeping pace with the market; its own leadership says the effect is underwhelming and that resourcing is stretched.

### Gaps / unknowns
- No published average or median duration for an OSA investigation.
- No published outcome measure linking age checks to reduced child access (Ofcom's passive measurement is a proxy).
- The 73% / 74% figures mix age assurance and geo-blocking, so the true "age-assured" rate is lower.
- Whether the 23 investigations include the 11 fined providers or are additional to them is not clear from the public text.

## 5. Self-reporting and weak assurance: a written record and a public statement, but no statutory audit or certification

**Description.** Section 81 requires a provider to keep a written record of what age-assurance it uses and to summarise that record publicly. It does not require independent audit, certification, testing, or publication of test results. Ofcom relies heavily on providers' own records and on its own sampling. The result is a compliance regime that can be satisfied by paperwork.

### What we found

- **The statutory duty is record-keeping plus a public statement — nothing more.** OSA 2023, s 81(4)–(5):
  > "(4) In relation to the duty set out in subsection (2), a duty to make and keep a written record, in an easily understandable form, of— (a) the kinds of age verification or age estimation used, and how they are used, and (b) the way in which the provider … has had regard to the importance of protecting United Kingdom users from a breach of any statutory provision or rule of law concerning privacy …
  > (5) A duty to summarise the written record in a publicly available statement, so far as the record concerns compliance with the duty set out in subsection (2), including details about which kinds of age verification or age estimation a provider is using and how they are used."
  — <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/81>

- **There is no certification requirement in the Act.** Section 82 allows Ofcom to recommend certification schemes in guidance; it does not require providers to be certified. Open Rights Group:
  > "While voluntary standards or schemes are insufficient to ensure security, privacy, and public confidence, they currently can be recommended by Ofcom (s.82 OSA) in their guidance, but it is not expected that Ofcom would have any role in shaping such a standard. … Making a scheme mandatory would require a change to the Online Safety Act."
  — <https://www.openrightsgroup.org/publications/regulating-age-verification>

- **Ofcom itself places the responsibility on the provider, not an auditor.** Its guidance says: "regardless of where the age assurance occurs in the ecosystem or whether it is implemented by the service provider or by a third-party, it is the responsibility of the regulated user-to-user service provider to ensure that age assurance is implemented in such a way that it is highly effective."
  — Quoted in Ofcom's Aylo investigation update, 23 Sept 2026: <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/enforcement-programme-to-protect-children-from-encountering-pornographic-content-through-the-use-of-age-assurance>

- **"Vet your vendor" is voluntary guidance, not audit.** Ofcom's October 2026 bulletin launches a "vet your vendor" resource to help providers "conduct regular due diligence" on third-party age-assurance vendors. It is explicitly a support tool, not a regulatory requirement or independent check.
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/online-safety-industry-bulletin-october-2026>

- **Independent "skilled persons" reports are not yet operational.** In October 2026 Ofcom was still running "preliminary market engagement" on the availability of "skilled persons" to provide reports under the Act, with a questionnaire deadline of 29 October 2026. That is the closest thing to an audit power, and it is not yet in use.
  — Ibid.

- **Ofcom's own assurance is sampling, not audit.** It says it "assessed over 1,000 pornography websites for the presence of age assurance" and uses "internal checks" and passive measurement. That is site-by-site observation of whether a gate appears, not a test of whether the gate works against a determined child.
  — IOS0039; Ofcom age assurance report.

- **The activity metric can be gamed.** 69 million "age checks" counts completed checks, not unique children blocked or attempts defeated. A site that prompts every visitor, including adults, inflates the number. Ofcom presents it as a 23-fold increase, not as an outcome.
  — Ofcom report, 15 July 2026.

### Status
**Structural.** The Act creates a documentary duty, not an assurance duty. Ofcom has no mandatory certification regime and its skilled-person audit mechanism is still being designed.

### Gaps / unknowns
- No published data on how many providers have been asked to produce their s 81(4) record, or how many records Ofcom has inspected.
- No published test of whether a sample of approved age-assurance methods can be defeated by a child with a parent's ID, a VPN, or a fake DOB.
- Whether Ofcom will make skilled-person reports mandatory for high-risk services, and on what timeline, is unknown.
- The privacy limb in s 81(4)(b) is a duty to "have regard", not a substantive security standard; no enforcement action on privacy grounds has been identified.

## 6. No individual redress: public enforcement only, no compensation route

**Description.** The OSA is a public-law regime. Ofcom does not investigate individual complaints, does not adjudicate individual cases, and cannot award compensation. A child or parent who says a site failed to age-check them has no statutory route to a remedy, and the fines go to the regulator/Treasury rather than to affected individuals.

### What we found

- **Ofcom's own statement is explicit.**
  > "We are unable to respond to or investigate individual complaints. However, we do use complaints to help us assess whether regulated services are appropriately protecting their users and if we should take any action."
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/online-safety-ofcom-role-and-what-it-means-for-you>

- **The complaints portal is intelligence-gathering, not casework.** The same page says complaints help Ofcom "assess whether regulated services are appropriately protecting their users and if we should take any action". There is no individual decision, reasons, or appeal.
  — Ibid.

- **Super-complaints are only for eligible bodies.** OSA s 169 allows "an eligible entity" — a body representing users — to make a super-complaint, and only where the complaint is of "particular importance" or affects "a particularly large number of users". An individual cannot use it.
  — <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/169>

- **Penalties are recoverable as court debts, not compensation.** Schedule 13 para 6 provides that a penalty is recoverable in England and Wales "if the county court so orders, as if it were payable under an order of that court" or "if the High Court so orders". The Act contains no provision for paying a penalty to a victim or funding redress.
  — <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/schedule/13>

- **No statutory ombudsman or compensation scheme.** No such scheme appears in the Act or in Ofcom's guidance. The only routes for an individual are general law (negligence, misuse of private information, data protection) or the ICO, neither of which is an OSA remedy.
  — Review of OSA 2023, Parts 3, 5, 7 and 8; no provision found.

- **The practical consequence.** The only people with standing to challenge an Ofcom decision are providers (s 168 appeals) and, on public-law grounds, anyone with sufficient interest for judicial review. A child who was able to access porn because a site had no age check has no direct party-and-party claim against Ofcom.

### Status
**Structural.** Public enforcement only; no individual redress, no compensation, no statutory victim fund.

### Gaps / unknowns
- Whether any private law claims have been brought against a porn provider for failing to age-assure (e.g. under data protection or misuse of private information) — none found in the public record.
- Whether the Government intends to create a redress scheme as part of the under-16 ban or the NCII reforms — no proposal found.
- The practical take-up of the super-complaints route (which bodies are "eligible") is not yet public.

## 7. Business disruption measures: strong on paper, never used, and procedurally clunky

**Description.** The Act gives Ofcom power to ask a court to order payment processors, advertisers, app stores, search engines and ISPs to cut off a non-compliant service. In practice the power requires continuing non-compliance, cannot be used for non-payment alone once a service has complied, has a high interim threshold, and has never been used. Ofcom is asking Parliament to rewrite it.

### What we found

- **The legal test for a service restriction order (s 144).** Ofcom may apply where:
  > "(a) the provider of the regulated service has failed to comply with an enforceable requirement …, (b) the failure is continuing, and (c) any of the following applies— (i) the provider has failed to comply with a requirement imposed by a confirmation decision …; (ii) the provider has failed to pay a penalty imposed by a confirmation decision relating to the failure (and the confirmation decision did not impose any requirements of a kind described in section 133(1)); (iii) the provider would be likely to fail to comply …; (iv) the circumstances … are such that it is appropriate to make the application without having given a provisional notice …".
  The court must be satisfied as to the grounds, that the target provides an "ancillary service", and that the order is "appropriate … for the purpose of preventing harm to individuals in the United Kingdom" and "proportionate to the risk of such harm" (s 144(6)).
  — <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/144>

- **Ancillary services expressly include payment providers, search engines, user-to-user services and ad servers/networks.** s 144(12). So the power is broad in theory.
  — Ibid.

- **The interim order threshold is high (s 145).** Ofcom must show it is "likely" the provider is failing, that the risk and severity of harm are such that "it would not be appropriate to wait", and that there are "prima facie grounds to suggest that an application for a service restriction order under section 144 would be successful" (s 145(3), (6)). Dawes says this in practice requires "a similar level of evidence as would be needed to secure a full order".
  — <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/145>; Griffiths letter, 6 Oct 2026.

- **Non-payment alone is not enough once a service complies.** Ofcom's public position:
  > "We do not have the legal powers to apply to block a site in the UK for failure to pay a fine alone. For us to be able to apply to a court for business disruption measures, there has to be continuing non-compliance with duties under the Online Safety Act."
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/what-happens-when-a-company-doesnt-pay-a-fine>

- **Cater explains the trap in terms.** "Because there is then no ongoing non-compliance, we are not able to apply for business disruption measures on the basis that they have not paid their penalty; that would be incredibly helpful."
  — Lords, 15 Sept 2026, <https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/18091/html/>

- **Dawes says she thought the power was wider.** "I thought we had more capability of going to BDMs, say, if people did not pay their fines. We do not, legally. If it is geo-blocked, as that site has been, we have exhausted our remedies under the Act."
  — HC 528, Q32.

- **Dawes calls the powers "very clunky".** "Those powers in the Act are very clunky; they take a very long time and do not really deal with the problem."
  — HC 528, Q32.

- **Ofcom prepared to apply in the suicide forum case, then did not.** On 13 May 2026 it said it was "preparing an application to the court for business disruption measures". On 20 July 2026 it concluded: "we have decided we cannot apply for such an order at this time because we do not consider the statutory grounds are met"; "all possible legal routes under the Act have now been exhausted"; and "neither Ofcom nor the UK courts have the power … to 'shut down' a website globally."
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/online-suicide-forum-investigation-and-review-of-enforcement-powers>

- **Ofcom has never obtained a BDM order.** No public record of any application, order, or contested hearing under ss 144–147 has been found. Legal commentary describes it as "the weapon that has not been fired".
  — Yair Cohen, 27 Sept 2026, <https://yaircohen.co.uk/the-hamster-the-judge-and-the-regulator/>

- **Ofcom's own reform asks (6 October 2026)** are a checklist of the weaknesses:
  - "Remove the requirement on Ofcom to show a continuing breach … and enable a pre-emptive order".
  - "Make it possible for Ofcom to secure BDMs for failure to pay a fine alone."
  - "Lower the threshold for securing interim business disruption orders."
  - Allow "dynamic orders" covering mirror sites without returning to court.
  - Consider "a new power that enables Ofcom to require a regulated service provider to take action on an interim basis in an urgent case".
  — Griffiths letter, 6 Oct 2026, <https://committees.parliament.uk/publications/55484/standard/pdf/>

- **Even the court route is slow by design.** Ofcom's letter concedes: "Even with the amendments detailed above, the BDM process remains court-led, which means that is not a process that enables particularly speedy action."
  — Ibid.

### Status
**Established.** Zero orders in 18 months. The only attempted use was abandoned because the provider geo-blocked, which removed the "continuing non-compliance" ground. Ofcom has formally asked for the test to be rewritten.

### Gaps / unknowns
- Whether any application was issued and then withdrawn, or whether the "preparing" stage never reached a filing.
- How long a contested s 144 application would take (no precedent).
- Whether payment processors or ad networks have voluntarily cut off any fined provider — no public data.
- Whether the Government will legislate in the current Parliament; no bill has been published as at 2 November 2026.

## 8. Corporate structuring, shell companies and cross-border enforcement

**Description.** The providers Ofcom is fining are often small, overseas, assetless, and deliberately structured to be hard to find. Some change corporate identity mid-investigation. Even a UK judgment debt may be unenforceable abroad, and US courts may refuse to enforce a foreign penal judgment. This is the deepest structural weakness: the Act's reach is broad, but its bite depends on assets and recognition.

### What we found

- **Ofcom's own description of the problem.**
  > "Some online service providers have deliberately structured themselves to try and avoid enforcement action – for example, by incorporating themselves in jurisdictions where limited information needs to be provided to the corporate register. Whether we are able to recover debt through assets in another country can also depend on whether a court in that country will recognise a UK court judgment, and whether we can identify individuals on whom we can serve proceedings."
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/what-happens-when-a-company-doesnt-pay-a-fine>

- **The same points are repeated in Ofcom's letter to the Lords.** It refers to "smaller services … the vast majority of which are based overseas, and from whom it is therefore more difficult to secure payment", and to "jurisdictions where limited information needs to be provided to the corporate register".
  — Griffiths letter, 6 Oct 2026.

- **Concrete example of identity-shifting: 8579 LLC → Reply Buzzer Ltd.** Ofcom's own decision records that, before the provisional decision, the provider named in the terms of service for 4kporn.xxx, crazyporn.xxx, hoes.tube and love4porn.com changed from 8579 LLC to Reply Buzzer Ltd. Ofcom therefore made no findings on 4kporn.xxx or 429.xxx, and opened a fresh investigation into Reply Buzzer. The £1.35m penalty attached to the old entity, while the sites continued under a new one.
  — <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/enforcement-programme-to-protect-children-from-encountering-pornographic-content-through-the-use-of-age-assurance>; Inforrm, 11 March 2026, <https://inforrm.org/2026/03/11/ofcom-steps-up-online-safety-act-enforcement-with-two-further-age-assurance-fines-for-pornographic-platforms-alexandros-antoniou/>

- **4chan: no UK staff, servers or assets.** Its lawyer, Preston Byrne, says 4chan has no UK office, employees, bank account or assets, so collection would require US proceedings. 4chan answered demand letters with an AI-generated hamster image and said it would comply "when pigs fly".
  — Dexerto, <https://www.dexerto.com/entertainment/ofcom-admits-it-may-never-collect-4chans-fines-after-ai-hamster-trolling-3420509>; Cohen analysis.

- **The US court has already signalled that collection will be contested.** On 15 September 2026, Judge Rudolph Contreras (D.D.C.) dismissed 4chan/Kiwi Farms' pre-emptive suit against Ofcom on FSIA grounds. But he went on, obiter, that US courts "are not necessarily required to enforce foreign penal judgments", citing a 1995 case refusing to enforce a British libel award on First Amendment grounds. The practical reading: 4chan cannot sue Ofcom in the US, but Ofcom may struggle to collect there.
  — *4chan Community Support LLC v Ofcom*, No. 1:25-cv-02880 (D.D.C.), 15 Sept 2026, <https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-dcd-1_25-cv-02880/pdf/USCOURTS-dcd-1_25-cv-02880-0.pdf>
  — ICLG, <https://iclg.com/news/ofcom-beats-us-lawsuit-over-online-safety-act-enforcement>

- **Service of notices is itself contested.** Ofcom served 197 s 100 notices on US businesses by February 2026, apparently by email rather than through the Hague Service Convention or the UK–US mutual legal assistance treaty. No court has ruled on whether email service is sufficient. This is a secondary source and should be treated with caution.
  — Reclaim The Net, April 2026, <https://reclaimthenet.org/ofcom-bypassed-us-treaty-to-send-197-censorship-orders> (secondary).

- **The fines are small relative to the operators' revenue.** The statutory maximum is £18m or 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue, but the largest fine to date is £1.4m. For a site earning advertising and subscription revenue from global traffic, a £1m fine may be a cost of doing business — particularly if it is never collected.
  — Sch 13 para 4, <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/schedule/13>; fine table above.

- **The enforcement model assumes economic incentives that do not exist for assetless foreign operators.** Ofcom's written evidence concedes: "The OSA is built on the assumption of services having economic and reputational incentives to engage constructively … But some services – particularly those that intend to do harm and which are often based outside the UK – will never respond constructively to regulation."
  — IOS0039, para 4.5.

### Status
**Structural and admitted.** Ofcom has no answer to an assetless foreign provider that is willing to be fined and not pay. Its own reform proposals do not include a cross-border collection mechanism; they focus on BDMs and on fining larger companies with UK assets.

### Gaps / unknowns
- How many of the 11 fined providers have no UK assets — Ofcom has not published this.
- Whether Ofcom has registered any judgment debt in the High Court, or instructed US counsel, is unknown.
- Whether the UK has any mutual recognition arrangement with the US or EU for regulatory penalties — none identified.
- The true ownership of several providers (8579 LLC, Reply Buzzer Ltd, Fapello, Xgroovy) is opaque; no beneficial-ownership data is public.

## 9. Litigation and appeals: the regulated can out-lawyer the regulator

**Description.** The biggest platforms challenge information notices, fees, categorisation and penalties. The fines regime is appealable to the Upper Tribunal on judicial-review principles, and providers have used every avenue. This slows enforcement, consumes Ofcom's legal resources, and creates a de facto advantage for well-funded defendants.

### What we found

- **Penalty appeals go to the Upper Tribunal.** OSA s 168 provides an appeal to the Upper Tribunal against, among other things, "a confirmation decision" and "a penalty notice", brought by "any person with a sufficient interest". The Tribunal applies "the same principles as would be applied by the High Court on an application for judicial review" and may quash the decision.
  — <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/168>

- **Youngtek is appealing its £600,000 penalty.** Ofcom's written evidence: "Youngtek have appealed our enforcement decision against it." This is the "one is being appealed" in Dawes's 20 October breakdown.
  — IOS0039, para 4.4.

- **Meta, X and TikTok lost their joint judicial review of Ofcom's information notices on 30 October 2026.** The High Court (Singh LJ and Collins Rice J) refused permission, holding that Ofcom's information-gathering powers served a distinct regulatory purpose and that the notices did not trigger the threshold for an overarching impact assessment. TikTok said it was "considering this judgment"; Meta said it was "disappointed".
  — <https://www.cityam.com/meta-and-tiktok-lose-high-court-battle-against-ofcoms-regulatory-data-demands/>

- **Meta is separately challenging the fees regime, twice.** Ofcom's Griffiths: "We currently have judicial reviews being run against us on our fees regime, twice, by Meta." Meta also lodged an Upper Tribunal appeal on 22 September 2026 against the Category 1 designation of WhatsApp and Instagram. Roblox and Quora are contesting their categorisations; four categorisation appeals are live.
  — Lords, 15 Sept 2026; IOS0039, para 4.4; Silicon UK, 22 Sept 2026, <https://www.silicon.co.uk/e-regulation/legal/meta-ofcom-legal-631659>

- **4chan's US suit failed on FSIA, but the collection fight is still ahead.** See section 8.
  — D.D.C. judgment, 15 Sept 2026.

- **The chilling effect is acknowledged.** Griffiths: "if we run unnecessary risks, we are going to get absolutely clobbered legally by deep-pocketed individuals and firms. We need to make sure that we have got the balance right."
  — Lords, 15 Sept 2026.

- **The Government has been urged to protect Ofcom from litigation costs.** Ofcom's chair says it must be "quite careful we are not taking on law cases where we will potentially lose and pay millions of people's legal fees." The costs regime for OSA appeals was consulted on in 2026; costs rules were retained rather than a broader costs-shifting regime.
  — HC 528, Q51; Pinsent Masons, <https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/costs-rules-stand-for-online-safety-act-appeals>

### Status
**Ongoing.** The information-notice JR has been lost by the platforms; the fees and categorisation challenges continue; Youngtek's penalty appeal is live. No successful penalty appeal has been reported.

### Gaps / unknowns
- The outcome and timetable of Youngtek's Upper Tribunal appeal.
- Whether any other fined provider has appealed but not announced it.
- The total legal spend by Ofcom on OSA litigation — not published.
- Whether the Upper Tribunal will hear penalty appeals on the merits or only on public-law grounds (the statutory test suggests the latter, which limits challenges but also limits correction of factual errors).

## 10. Other enforcement weaknesses worth flagging

**Description.** Several cross-cutting features amplify the weaknesses above: the fines are modest, the money goes to the state rather than victims, the largest platforms have not yet been fined, and the ancillary-service levers (payments, advertising, search) remain untested.

### What we found

- **No Category 1 platform has been fined.** As at October 2026, all fines are against porn operators, 4chan, a nudification app, a file-sharing site and a suicide forum. The first Category 1 fine may come from the X/Grok or TikTok investigations, both still open.
  — Cohen analysis; Ofcom case pages.

- **Search engines are not required to age-assure.** Ofcom found 33% of first-page Google results and 54% of Bing results were to porn sites without age checks or other protections. Ofcom can only "work with" Google and Bing; the Act does not impose a HEAA duty on search for this purpose.
  — Ofcom report, 15 July 2026, note 8; press release.

- **Payment and advertising cut-off is untested.** The BDM power expressly covers payment processors, ad servers and ad networks (s 144(12)), but no order has been sought. Whether Visa/Mastercard or major ad networks would comply voluntarily is unknown.
  — s 144(12); no public record.

- **Mirror sites and domain-hopping.** Ofcom's reform letter asks for "dynamic orders" that can be expanded to cover "new sites with the same content/branding (such as mirror sites) without having to go back to the Court". That is an admission that the current order-by-order approach cannot keep up.
  — Griffiths letter, 6 Oct 2026.

- **Daily penalties may be unenforceable in practice.** Several decisions impose daily rates (e.g. £1,000/day for 8579, £500/day for 4chan, £200/day for Fapello/Xgroovy). If the underlying fixed penalty is unpaid and the provider is overseas, the daily rate is likely to suffer the same fate. No published total of accrued daily penalties has been found.
  — Decision pages.

- **Fines are not hypothecated to victims or enforcement.** Ofcom says "We do not rely on the fines. We rely on the fees." The fines are a "bonus to the Treasury" in Sir Ian Cheshire's phrase. That weakens the argument that enforcement is funded by the industry it regulates.
  — HC 528, Q63–Q64.

- **The "3 still available" figure is the sharpest public metric of failure.** Of the 11 providers fined, three services remain accessible in the UK with risks unmitigated, according to Dawes. That is the residue after fines, geo-blocks and compliance — and it is the number that most directly undermines the deterrent claim.
  — HC 528, Q59.

### Status
**Mixed / ongoing.** Some levers are untested rather than proven ineffective; others (search, mirror sites, daily penalties) are known gaps.

### Gaps / unknowns
- Whether Ofcom has referred any provider to law enforcement for criminal liability (senior-manager liability) — Cater says it is "starting to use" the power, but no case is public.
- Whether any payment processor has voluntarily withdrawn services from a fined provider.
- The number of mirror sites operated by the fined providers.
- Whether the forthcoming under-16 ban will change the enforcement arithmetic by bringing the largest platforms into the fines population.

## Cross-cutting assessment

The evidence supports four propositions:

1. **The fines are real but the money is not.** Ofcom has issued 31 fines worth over £7m and collected £135,000 (1.9%). The regulator itself says it will not recover every fine. A penalty regime that collects one-fiftieth of its nominal value cannot credibly deter an assetless foreign operator.

2. **Geo-blocking has become the functional equivalent of compliance.** Ofcom counts geo-blocked sites in its success metrics, closes investigations without findings when a site blocks the UK, and describes geo-blocking as a "win". A service can therefore exit the UK market rather than protect UK children, and still be counted as a success — while VPN users continue to access it.

3. **The coercive machinery is unused.** Business disruption measures — the one tool that does not depend on the provider's cooperation or assets — have never been used. The only attempt was abandoned because the provider geo-blocked, which removed the "continuing non-compliance" ground. Ofcom has asked Parliament to rewrite the test.

4. **The regime is public-enforcement only.** There is no individual redress, no compensation, no statutory audit or certification, and no outcome-based measure of success. The metrics that exist (69 million checks, 64/100 sites, 73% of 88 services) measure activity and paper compliance, not whether children are actually protected. The Children's Commissioner's "absolutely no difference" and Ofcom's own "underwhelmed" are the clearest evidence that the gap between activity and outcome is now acknowledged at the highest levels.

## Method and caveats

- **Dates.** All dates are 2026 unless otherwise stated. The research cut-off is 2 November 2026.
- **Fine totals.** The £6.885m table total is calculated from the published fixed penalties. Ofcom's "over £7 million" figure may include daily-rate penalties, later decisions, or rounding. The 11-provider count is inferred from Ofcom's own list plus im.ge and the suicide forum; Ofcom has not published a single consolidated table.
- **Payment status.** Only Itai Tech and First Time Videos are confirmed paid by Ofcom. "No" means no public confirmation of payment, not proof of non-payment. Ofcom has refused FOI requests for a provider-by-provider payment list.
- **Quotations.** Quotes are taken from the published transcripts and press releases cited. Where a transcript is a "corrected" version, the quote is from that version. Ellipses mark omitted words; square brackets mark editorial clarification.
- **Secondary sources.** The Reclaim The Net claim about 197 s 100 notices served by email is secondary and has not been independently verified; it is flagged as such in section 8.
- **4chan.** Deliberately treated briefly; the separate subagent's deep dive should be preferred for the FSIA analysis, the hamster correspondence, and the procedural history.
